Overview

Evidence

  • Improves the quality of decisions.
  • Helps you use evidence effectively, not just collect it.
  • Reduces bias and errors in reasoning.
  • Strengthens arguments and credibility.
  • Evidence is central to decision making.
  • Quality matters more than quantity.
  • Strong decisions use multiple sources of evidence.

  • Even good evidence has limitations that must be considered.

Developing Keywords for a Research Question

Research Question

Does the four-day work week improve employee well-being?

Before searching for scholarly articles, identify the main keywords in your research question. Then, brainstorm alternative search terms that authors might use to discuss the same concepts.

1. Identify Keywords and Search Terms

A keyword represents an important concept in your research question. Search terms are alternative words or phrases that you can use to find articles about that concept.

Keyword Alternative Search Terms
Four-day work week "four-day workweek," "4-day work week," "compressed workweek," "reduced workweek," "shorter workweek," "reduced working hours"
Employee "employees," "workers," "working adults," "workforce," "personnel," "staff"
Well-being "employee well-being," "employee wellbeing," "psychological well-being," "workplace well-being," "mental health," "life satisfaction," "work-life balance"
Improve "improvement," "effects," "impact," "influence," "outcomes," "benefits," "association"
Research Tip: You do not need to use every keyword in every search. Start with the main concepts, then add or remove terms depending on the articles you find.

2. Combine Search Terms

Research databases allow you to combine keywords using Boolean operators. These operators help you control which articles appear in your results.

OR: Search for Alternatives

Use OR between alternative search terms for the same concept. This broadens your search because an article can contain any of the listed terms.

"four-day workweek" OR "four-day work week" OR "compressed workweek" OR "reduced working hours"

AND: Combine Different Concepts

Use AND to connect different concepts. This narrows your search to articles that address both the work schedule and employee well-being.

("four-day workweek" OR "compressed workweek") AND ("employee well-being" OR "mental health" OR "work-life balance")

Search for Empirical Research

Add terms related to research studies when you want to find articles that evaluate outcomes or report collected data.

("four-day workweek" OR "reduced working hours") AND (well-being OR "mental health" OR "job satisfaction") AND (study OR experiment OR evaluation)

Recommended Search Strategy

  1. Start broad. Search for four-day workweeks and alternative work schedules to understand the existing research.
  2. Add well-being terms. Include employee well-being, mental health, stress, burnout, job satisfaction, and work-life balance.
  3. Look for measurable outcomes. Search for studies that measure employee outcomes before and after a schedule change or compare employees working different schedules.
  4. Check the type of workweek. Read each article carefully to determine whether employees work fewer total hours or compress their usual hours into four days.
Important Distinction: A compressed workweek typically fits the usual working hours into fewer, longer days. A reduced-hours four-day workweek decreases the total number of hours worked. These are different interventions and may produce different employee well-being outcomes.

Where Should You Look?

  • Google Scholar for academic studies and scholarly books.
  • Government publications for reports, guidance, and statistics.
  • Professional organizations for standards and industry reports.
  • Public case studies describing organizational implementations and outcomes.
  • University libraries for access to research databases.

5. Evaluate Whether an Article Supports Your Premise

Finding an article about a topic does not automatically mean it supports your argument.

  1. Relevance: Does it directly address your premise?
  2. Credibility: Who produced the information and what expertise do they have?
  3. Evidence: What data or documentation supports the findings?
  4. Limitations: What conditions affect the results?
  5. Accuracy: Does the source actually support the statement you want to make?
Look for evidence that matches the specific point you need to establish rather than assuming a source supports your argument because it discusses the topic.

Organize research materials around the structure of your argument rather than storing all sources in a single folder.

Create a research tracker to record the role each source plays in your argument.

8. What If an Article Supports More Than One Premise?

Research rarely fits neatly into a single category. One article may contain multiple findings relevant to multiple premises.

  • Identify the specific finding that supports each premise.
  • Record page numbers, tables, figures, or sections.
  • Avoid treating every finding in an article as support for every premise.
  • Revise premises when evidence suggests a better formulation.

9. When Should You Stop Searching?

Research is iterative, but a premise is typically ready for the next stage when you can:

  • Explain the statement clearly and specifically.
  • Identify credible sources that directly address it.
  • Describe findings accurately.
  • Explain important limitations.
  • Recognize disagreements or contradictory findings.
  • Connect the evidence to the subclaim.
Your goal is not to collect the largest number of articles. Your goal is to gather enough credible evidence to evaluate the premise responsibly.

10. Your Research Workflow

  1. Select a subclaim.
  2. Identify supporting premises.
  3. Turn premises into research questions.
  4. Search for credible sources.
  5. Review evidence and limitations.
  6. Organize materials by premise.
  7. Update your research tracker.
  8. Reassess and revise premises as needed.
  9. Repeat for the next premise.

Overview

Making a decision requires evidence. However, evidence is not about quantity—it is about quality. You need to know what kind of evidence to use, when to use it, and why it matters. The sources, types, and quality of evidence strongly shape the decisions we make.

Evidence is not just information. It is a strategic and ethical resource that supports valid and defensible decisions. In EEBDM, the main challenge is usually not finding information but using it effectively. Tools such as AI can help organize and analyze evidence, but they cannot replace human judgment.

Evidence: Any information that can influence decision making, including preferences, attitudes, experience, data, and anticipated consequences.

The Four Sources of Evidence

Strong arguments draw from multiple types of evidence. Ideally, you should use all four sources when supporting a decision.

Source Definition
Expert Opinion An informed judgment from someone with relevant expertise and experience.
Stakeholder Opinion The perspective of individuals affected by the decision.
Internal Data Data from within the organization (financials, performance, customer data).
External Data Data from outside sources (research, competitors, market reports).

Evaluating Evidence (CRAAP Test)

Why Credibility Matters: Because there is so much information available, evaluating credibility is essential for making strong, evidence-based decisions.
Criteria Key Question Explanation
Currency Is it up to date? Recent information is more useful, especially in fast-changing areas.
Relevance Does it apply to my decision? Evidence must directly address your specific question.
Authority Who created it? Sources should have proper expertise or credentials.
Accuracy Is it reliable? Information should be supported by evidence and consistent across sources.
Purpose Why was it created? Be aware of bias, intent, and possible agendas.

Evidence Limitations

Even strong evidence has limitations. Understanding these helps you avoid common reasoning mistakes.

Concept Explanation
Correlation ≠ Causation Just because two things are related does not mean one causes the other.
Sample Bias Results may be misleading if the sample does not represent the full population.
Ecological Fallacy Applying general trends to specific cases can lead to incorrect conclusions.
Overgeneralization Drawing broad conclusions from limited or narrow evidence.
P-Value vs Effect Size Statistical significance does not mean the effect is meaningful in practice.
Lying with Data Graphs and visuals can be misleading if poorly designed or manipulated.
