Types of Evidence

Overview

Evidence is essential to a strong argument. An argument may sound logical, but without evidence to support its claims, it has little persuasive strength. However, more evidence does not necessarily make an argument stronger. The goal is to use an appropriate amount of high-quality evidence that directly supports the argument. Too much evidence can overwhelm readers and make it harder for them to identify the most important points.

  • Evidence can improve the quality of decisions.
  • Careful evaluation can reduce bias and errors in reasoning.
  • Credible evidence strengthens arguments and their defensibility.
  • Multiple sources can provide a more complete picture.
  • Quality, relevance, and limitations matter more than quantity alone.

Finding Articles

Step 1: Transform Premises into Research Questions

Once you identify a potential premise, turn it into a research question. A clear question helps you identify what information to search for and which sources are relevant.

Potential premise: A four-day workweek can improve employee well-being.

Research question: Does a four-day workweek improve employee well-being?

Step 2: Identify the Keywords in the Research Question

A keyword represents an important concept in your research question. Search terms are alternative words or phrases authors might use to discuss that concept.

Research Question: Does a four-day workweek improve employee well-being?

Keyword Alternative Search Terms
Four-day workweek "four-day work week," "4-day workweek," "compressed workweek," "reduced workweek," "shorter workweek," "reduced working hours"
Employee employees, workers, working adults, workforce, personnel, staff
Well-being "employee well-being," "employee wellbeing," "psychological well-being," "workplace well-being," "mental health," "life satisfaction," "work-life balance"
Improve improvement, effects, impact, influence, outcomes, benefits, association
Research Tip: You do not need to use every term in every search. Start with the main concepts, then adjust your search based on the articles you find.

Overview

Step 3: Search for Articles Using the Keywords

Recommended research databases include:

  • Google Scholar for academic studies and scholarly books.
  • University library databases for scholarly articles and research reports.
  • Government publications for reports, guidance, and statistics.
  • Professional organizations for standards and industry reports.
  • Public case studies describing organizational implementations and outcomes.

Tips for Combining Keywords

Use OR to include alternative terms and AND to combine different concepts.

OR: Find Alternative Terms

"four-day workweek" OR "compressed workweek" OR "reduced working hours"
AND: Combine Concepts

("four-day workweek" OR "compressed workweek") AND ("employee well-being" OR "mental health" OR "job satisfaction")
Find Research Studies

("four-day workweek" OR "reduced working hours") AND ("employee well-being" OR "mental health") AND (study OR experiment OR evaluation)

Recommended Search Strategy

  1. Start broad. Search for four-day workweeks and alternative work schedules.
  2. Add related terms. Try mental health, stress, burnout, job satisfaction, and work-life balance.
  3. Look for research. Find studies that measure employee outcomes or compare different work schedules.
  4. Check the work hours. Determine whether employees work fewer total hours or fit their usual hours into four longer days.

When Should You Stop Searching?

A premise is ready for the next stage when you can:

  • Explain the statement clearly and specifically.
  • Identify credible sources that directly address it.
  • Describe findings accurately.
  • Explain important limitations.
  • Recognize disagreement or contradictory findings.
  • Explain how the evidence connects to the subclaim.
Your goal is not to collect every possible article on the topic. Your goal is to gather enough credible evidence to evaluate the premise responsibly.

Evaluating Evidence Quality

Finding an article about a topic does not automatically mean it supports your argument. Evaluate each source in relation to the specific statement you need to establish.

Evaluate Articles Based Relevance, Credibility, and Evidence

Step 1: Is this article relevant?

Is this article relevant to the premise? The article must provide information that directly relates to the specific premise you are trying to support. An article can discuss the same general topic without providing evidence for your particular premise. Focus on what the article actually investigates, measures, or explains. Does the article address my premise, or does it only discuss the general topic?

Step 2: Is the author/ publisher of this article credible?

The author should have relevant expertise in the subject, such as a degree within the field they are discussing, professional credentials, or substantial experience in the field. Their level of expertise should be sufficient for the complexity and depth of the topic they are discussing. The publisher should have a reputation for providing reliable information. Consider whether the article was peer-reviewed, whether the author explains their sources, and whether the publisher follows standards for accuracy. Also consider potential conflicts of interest or financial motivations that could influence how the information is presented. A potential conflict of interest does not automatically make an article unreliable, but it is a reason to evaluate the evidence carefully.

Ask yourself: Who created this information, what qualifies them to discuss the topic, and do they have a reason to present the information in a particular way?

Does this article provide enough evidence?

The article must provide support for its statements, such as research findings, data, documented observations, established standards, or clearly explained methods. An author's opinion or an unsupported statement is not enough to establish that a premise is accurate. Examine how the evidence was collected, what the findings actually show, and whether limitations affect the conclusions you can draw. For example, a study may find that two variables are related without demonstrating that one causes the other.

Ask yourself: What evidence supports the article's conclusions, and are there any limitations that prevent me from using those findings to support my premise?

Even a credible article that is relevant to your topic may have limitations. These limitations affect how confidently you can use its findings and what conclusions you can reasonably draw. A limitation does not automatically make an article low quality. The key is to understand how the limitation affects the conclusions you can draw and whether the evidence is still appropriate for supporting your specific premise.

Concept Explanation
Correlation vs. Causation Two variables can be related without one causing the other. For example, a study may find that employees who work shorter weeks report greater well-being, but this does not automatically prove that shorter workweeks caused the improvement.
Sampling Bias A research sample may differ systematically from the population the researcher wants to understand. For example, a survey about employee well-being that includes only volunteers may not represent all employees.
Ecological Fallacy Group-level patterns do not necessarily apply to individuals. For example, finding that companies with shorter workweeks have higher productivity does not prove that every employee is more productive under that schedule.
Overgeneralization An author draws conclusions that go beyond what the evidence supports. For example, a study of employees at one company may not justify a conclusion about all industries or workplaces.
Statistical Significance vs. Effect Size A statistically significant finding is not necessarily large or practically important. For example, a study may find a statistically significant improvement in job satisfaction, but the actual improvement may be very small.
Misleading Data Displays Graphs and visualizations can distort interpretation through truncated axes, missing labels, unclear denominators, or inappropriate scales. Check whether the display presents the data accurately and provides enough context to interpret the results.
Out-of-Date Information Information may no longer accurately reflect current conditions, standards, technologies, or practices. Whether a source is too old depends on the field and research question. For example, older research may still be useful for established psychological theories, while cybersecurity recommendations may need to reflect current threats and technologies.
