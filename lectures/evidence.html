Types of Evidence

Overview

Evidence is essential to a strong argument. An argument may sound logical, but without evidence to support its claims, it has little persuasive strength. However, more evidence does not necessarily make an argument stronger. The goal is to use an appropriate amount of high-quality evidence that directly supports the argument. Too much evidence can overwhelm readers and make it harder for them to identify the most important points.

  • Evidence can improve the quality of decisions.
  • Careful evaluation can reduce bias and errors in reasoning.
  • Credible evidence strengthens arguments and their defensibility.
  • Multiple sources can provide a more complete picture.
  • Quality, relevance, and limitations matter more than quantity alone.

Part 1: Finding Articles (Quantity)

Transform Premises to Research Questions

Once you identify a potential premise, turn it into a research question. A clear question helps you identify what information to search for and which sources are relevant.

For example:

Potential premise: A four-day workweek can improve employee well-being.
Research question: Does a four-day workweek improve employee well-being?

Identify the Keywords in the Research Question

A keyword represents an important concept in your research question. Search terms are alternative words or phrases authors might use to discuss that concept.

Research Question

Does a four-day workweek improve employee well-being?

KeywordAlternative Search Terms
Four-day workweek"four-day work week," "4-day workweek," "compressed workweek," "reduced workweek," "shorter workweek," "reduced working hours"
Employeeemployees, workers, working adults, workforce, personnel, staff
Well-being"employee well-being," "employee wellbeing," "psychological well-being," "workplace well-being," "mental health," "life satisfaction," "work-life balance"
Improveimprovement, effects, impact, influence, outcomes, benefits, association
Research tip: You do not need to use every term in every search. Start with the main concepts, then adjust your search based on the articles you find.

Search for Articles Using the Keywords

Recommended research databases include:

  • Google Scholar for academic studies and scholarly books.
  • University library databases for scholarly articles and research reports.
  • Government publications for reports, guidance, and statistics.
  • Professional organizations for standards and industry reports.
  • Public case studies describing organizational implementations and outcomes.

Tips for Combining Keywords

Use OR to include alternative terms and AND to combine different concepts.

OR: Find alternative terms

"four-day workweek" OR "compressed workweek" OR "reduced working hours"

AND: Combine concepts

("four-day workweek" OR "compressed workweek") AND ("employee well-being" OR "mental health" OR "job satisfaction")

Find research studies

("four-day workweek" OR "reduced working hours") AND ("employee well-being" OR "mental health") AND (study OR experiment OR evaluation)

Recommended Search Strategy

  1. Start broad. Search for four-day workweeks and alternative work schedules.
  2. Add related terms. Try mental health, stress, burnout, job satisfaction, and work-life balance.
  3. Look for research. Find studies that measure employee outcomes or compare different work schedules.
  4. Check the work hours. Determine whether employees work fewer total hours or fit their usual hours into four longer days.

Briefly Evaluate the Article: Keep or Discard

Evaluate Whether an Article Supports Your Premise

Finding an article about a topic does not automatically mean it supports your argument. Evaluate each source in relation to the specific statement you need to establish.

  1. Relevance: Does the source directly address your premise?
  2. Credibility: Who produced the information, and what expertise do they have?
  3. Evidence: What data, methods, or documentation support the findings?
  4. Limitations: What conditions, sample characteristics, or methods limit how much you can apply the results of the article to your argument?
  5. Accuracy: Does the source actually support the statement you want to make?
Use the specific findings that support your point. Do not assume a source supports your argument simply because it discusses the same topic.

When Should You Stop Searching?

A premise is ready for the next stage when you can:

  • Explain the statement clearly and specifically.
  • Identify credible sources that directly address it.
  • Describe the findings accurately.
  • Explain important limitations.
  • Recognize disagreement or contradictory findings.
  • Explain how the evidence connects to the subclaim.
Your goal is not to collect the largest number of articles. Your goal is to gather enough credible evidence to evaluate the premise responsibly.

The Four Sources of Evidence

Evidence-based decision making can draw on different sources. Use the sources that are relevant and accessible, and evaluate their quality rather than assuming that every source is equally strong.

SourceDefinitionExample
Expert opinionAn informed judgment from someone with relevant expertise or experience.A cybersecurity specialist explains common attack methods.
Stakeholder opinionThe perspectives of people affected by the decision.Employees describe barriers to completing security training.
Internal dataInformation collected within an organization.Aggregated training completion rates or incident counts, when authorized and available.
External dataInformation from outside the organization.Peer-reviewed research, government statistics, or industry reports.

Evaluating Evidence: The CRAAP Test

Why credibility matters: Because information is abundant, you need to evaluate whether a source is current, relevant, authoritative, accurate, and appropriate for your purpose.
CriteriaKey QuestionExplanation
CurrencyIs it up to date?Recency matters especially in fast-changing fields. Older foundational research may still be valuable.
RelevanceDoes it apply to my question?The evidence should address the claim, population, setting, or outcome you are investigating.
AuthorityWho created it?Consider the author's expertise, the publisher, and the source's review process.
AccuracyIs it supported and verifiable?Check methods, citations, data quality, and whether other credible sources corroborate the claim.
PurposeWhy was it created?Consider the intended audience, potential conflicts of interest, and possible persuasive or commercial goals.

Evidence Limitations

Even credible evidence has limitations. Consider these issues before drawing conclusions or recommending action.

ConceptExplanation
Correlation does not establish causationTwo variables may be related without one causing the other. Other variables or reverse causation may explain the relationship.
Sampling biasA sample may systematically differ from the population you want to understand.
Ecological fallacyGroup-level patterns do not necessarily describe individuals within those groups.
OvergeneralizationA conclusion may go beyond what the sample, setting, or study design can support.
Statistical significance versus effect sizeA statistically significant result is not necessarily large or practically important.
Misleading data displaysTruncated axes, unclear denominators, selective reporting, or inappropriate visual scales can distort interpretation.
