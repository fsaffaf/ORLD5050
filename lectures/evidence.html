Types of Evidence

Overview

Making a decision requires evidence. However, evidence is not about quantity—it is about quality. You need to know what kind of evidence to use, when to use it, and why it matters. The sources, types, and quality of evidence strongly shape the decisions we make.

Evidence is not just information. It is a strategic and ethical resource that supports valid and defensible decisions. In EEBDM, the main challenge is usually not finding information but using it effectively. Tools such as AI can help organize and analyze evidence, but they cannot replace human judgment.

Evidence: Any information that can influence decision making, including preferences, attitudes, experience, data, and anticipated consequences.

The Four Sources of Evidence

Strong arguments draw from multiple types of evidence. Ideally, you should use all four sources when supporting a decision.

Evaluating Evidence (CRAAP Test)

Why Credibility Matters: Because there is so much information available, evaluating credibility is essential for making strong, evidence-based decisions.
Criteria Key Question Explanation
Currency Is it up to date? Recent information is more useful, especially in fast-changing areas.
Relevance Does it apply to my decision? Evidence must directly address your specific question.
Authority Who created it? Sources should have proper expertise or credentials.
Accuracy Is it reliable? Information should be supported by evidence and consistent across sources.
Purpose Why was it created? Be aware of bias, intent, and possible agendas.

Evidence Limitations

Even strong evidence has limitations. Understanding these helps you avoid common reasoning mistakes.

Concept Explanation
Correlation ≠ Causation Just because two things are related does not mean one causes the other.
Sample Bias Results may be misleading if the sample does not represent the full population.
Ecological Fallacy Applying general trends to specific cases can lead to incorrect conclusions.
Overgeneralization Drawing broad conclusions from limited or narrow evidence.
P-Value vs Effect Size Statistical significance does not mean the effect is meaningful in practice.
Lying with Data Graphs and visuals can be misleading if poorly designed or manipulated.

Why This Matters

  • Improves the quality of decisions.
  • Helps you use evidence effectively, not just collect it.
  • Reduces bias and errors in reasoning.
  • Strengthens arguments and credibility.

Key Takeaways

  • Evidence is central to decision making.
  • Quality matters more than quantity.
  • Strong decisions use multiple sources of evidence.
  • All evidence should be evaluated for credibility.
  • Even good evidence has limitations that must be considered.
