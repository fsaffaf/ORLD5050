Ethics in Arguments

Overview

Ethics is the process of thinking about what is right, wrong, fair, and responsible. In organizations, ethical decisions involve considering how workplace policies, leadership choices, and business practices affect employees, customers, communities, and the organization itself.

Organizations make decisions every day that involve ethical questions. For example:

  • Should an organization monitor employees' computer activity?
  • Should employees be required to return to the office?
  • Should a company use artificial intelligence to make hiring decisions?
  • Should an organization prioritize employee satisfaction or cost reduction?
  • Should managers share information with employees when a major organizational change is being considered?

These decisions are not always simple. A decision might benefit the organization financially but negatively affect employees. A policy might improve productivity but raise concerns about privacy or fairness.

Ethical perspectives provide different ways to evaluate these decisions. Rather than relying only on personal opinions, individuals can use ethical reasoning to explain why a decision is appropriate or inappropriate.

The three perspectives can be used to evaluate the same organizational decision. However, each perspective focuses on a different part of the ethical question.

Ethical Perspective Main Focus Central Question
Consequences Outcomes and effects Does this decision produce more benefits than harm?
Rights and Fairness Rights, responsibilities, and equal treatment Does this decision respect people and treat them fairly?
Character Values and ethical leadership Does this decision demonstrate honesty, integrity, and responsibility?

Consider an organization deciding whether to require employees to return to the office.

  • Consequences: Will returning to the office improve collaboration and productivity? Could it increase commuting costs, stress, or employee turnover?
  • Rights and Fairness: Are employees being treated consistently? Are individual circumstances and legitimate needs being considered?
  • Character: Is leadership communicating honestly about the reasons for the policy? Is the organization demonstrating respect and responsibility toward employees?

Notice that the three perspectives do not necessarily produce the same answer. One perspective might emphasize organizational benefits, while another might raise concerns about fairness or employee treatment.

Using all three perspectives helps individuals evaluate a decision more completely.

Consequences

The consequences perspective focuses on the results of a decision. It asks whether a decision produces more benefits than harm for the people and groups affected.

When using this perspective, consider both the immediate and long-term outcomes. A decision might solve an immediate organizational problem but create additional problems over time.

Questions to consider:

  • Who benefits from this decision?
  • Who might be negatively affected?
  • Will the decision improve or harm employee well-being?
  • Will it improve organizational performance?
  • What are the possible short-term and long-term effects?
  • Do the overall benefits outweigh the potential harm?

Organizational Example: Employee Monitoring

Imagine an organization is considering monitoring employees' computer activity to improve productivity.

From a consequences perspective, you would consider the potential benefits and disadvantages.

Potential benefits include:

  • Identifying inefficient work processes.
  • Improving information security.
  • Identifying employees who need additional support.
  • Increasing accountability.

Potential disadvantages include:

  • Reducing employee trust.
  • Increasing workplace stress.
  • Making employees feel that they are constantly being watched.
  • Encouraging employees to focus on appearing busy rather than doing meaningful work.

The ethical question is whether the expected benefits justify the potential negative effects.

How to Use This Perspective in an Argument

You might argue that an organization should limit employee monitoring because excessive monitoring can reduce employee trust and increase workplace stress. Research about employee satisfaction, workplace trust, and productivity could provide evidence to support this position.

Your reasoning would explain why an organization should consider the overall effects of its policies rather than focusing only on productivity.

Rights & Fairness

The rights and fairness perspective focuses on whether decisions respect individuals and treat people fairly. It considers the responsibilities organizations have toward employees, customers, and other stakeholders.

People have legitimate interests and rights that organizations should consider when making decisions. These may include privacy, safety, equal treatment, respectful communication, and fair opportunities.

Fairness also means considering whether policies are applied consistently and whether individuals are treated appropriately.

A decision is not necessarily ethical simply because it benefits the majority or improves organizational performance. Organizations must also consider whether the decision respects the people affected by it.

Questions to consider:

  • Does this decision respect people's rights?
  • Are people being treated fairly?
  • Are policies applied consistently?
  • Are employees given equal opportunities?
  • Are people being treated with dignity and respect?
  • Are individuals being excluded, disadvantaged, or treated unfairly?
  • Does the organization have a responsibility to protect the people affected by this decision?

Organizational Example: Artificial Intelligence in Hiring

Imagine an organization wants to use artificial intelligence to screen job applicants.

From a rights and fairness perspective, you would consider whether the technology treats applicants fairly.

For example:

  • Does the system evaluate applicants using job-related qualifications?
  • Could the system unfairly disadvantage certain applicants?
  • Are applicants informed about how their information is being used?
  • Can applicants question or challenge an unfair decision?
  • Does the organization provide a fair opportunity for qualified individuals?

Even if artificial intelligence saves time and reduces hiring costs, the organization must consider whether the process respects applicants and provides fair opportunities.

How to Use This Perspective in an Argument

You might argue that organizations should regularly evaluate artificial intelligence hiring systems to identify unfair outcomes.

Your evidence could include research about algorithmic bias, hiring practices, or employment discrimination. Your reasoning would explain why organizations have a responsibility to use hiring practices that respect applicants and provide fair opportunities.

Character

The character perspective focuses on the values and qualities demonstrated through a person's or organization's decisions.

It asks what an ethical person, manager, or organization would do. Rather than focusing only on outcomes or rules, this perspective considers the character behind a decision.

Important ethical qualities include:

  • Honesty: Communicating truthfully and avoiding deception.
  • Integrity: Doing what is right, even when it is difficult or inconvenient.
  • Responsibility: Accepting accountability for decisions and their effects.
  • Respect: Treating others with dignity and consideration.
  • Transparency: Being open about decisions, policies, and relevant information.
  • Trustworthiness: Acting in ways that others can depend on.

In organizations, ethical character is demonstrated through leadership decisions, communication, workplace policies, and everyday interactions.

Questions to consider:

  • Is the decision honest and transparent?
  • Does it demonstrate integrity?
  • Is the organization accepting responsibility for its actions?
  • Would an ethical leader make this decision?
  • Does the decision demonstrate respect for employees and customers?
  • Does the organization follow its stated values?
  • Would the organization be comfortable explaining this decision publicly?

Organizational Example: Communicating Organizational Changes

Imagine an organization plans to eliminate several positions because of financial difficulties. Leadership knows that employees will be affected but delays communicating the decision to avoid uncomfortable conversations.

From a character perspective, you would consider whether leadership is demonstrating honesty, integrity, and responsibility.

An ethical approach would involve communicating relevant information honestly, treating affected employees respectfully, and accepting responsibility for the organization's decisions.

Even when a decision is financially necessary, the way leadership communicates and handles the situation matters.

How to Use This Perspective in an Argument

You might argue that organizations should communicate major workplace changes transparently, even when the information is difficult to share.

Your evidence could include research about organizational trust, employee communication, and leadership. Your reasoning would explain why honesty and responsibility are important qualities of ethical leadership.

How Ethics Connects to Organizational Arguments

Ethical perspectives are especially useful when developing arguments about workplace decisions. They help you explain not only what you believe an organization should do, but also why that decision is appropriate.

In this course, you will use a Toulmin-style argument structure consisting of four main components:

  1. Claim: What position are you arguing for?
  2. Evidence: What research, facts, statistics, or expert information support your position?
  3. Warrant: Why does your evidence support your claim?
  4. Rebuttal: What is a reasonable opposing argument, and how can you respond to it?

Ethical perspectives can help you develop each part of your argument, particularly your warrant.

1. Claim: Identify Your Position

Your claim states what you believe an organization should do.

For example:

Organizations should establish clear limits on employee monitoring to protect employee privacy and maintain workplace trust.

Your claim identifies a specific organizational action.

2. Evidence: Find Information to Support Your Position

Evidence provides factual information that supports your claim.

For example, you might find research about:

  • Employee monitoring and workplace stress.
  • The relationship between employee trust and productivity.
  • Workplace privacy policies.
  • Organizational security practices.

Evidence should come from credible sources, such as scientific research, government publications, professional standards, and industry reports.

Ethical perspectives can help you determine what types of evidence are relevant to your argument.

For example, if you are using the consequences perspective, you might look for research about the effects of employee monitoring on productivity and well-being.

If you are using the rights and fairness perspective, you might look for information about workplace privacy and employee rights.

If you are using the character perspective, you might look for research about transparency, trust, and ethical leadership.

3. Warrant: Explain Why Your Evidence Supports Your Claim

The warrant connects your evidence to your claim. It explains why the information you found supports the organizational action you are recommending.

This is where ethical reasoning becomes especially important.

For example:

Claim: Organizations should establish clear limits on employee monitoring.

Evidence: Research indicates that excessive workplace monitoring can negatively affect employee trust and increase stress.

Warrant: Organizations have a responsibility to consider how workplace policies affect employee well-being and trust. Policies that create unnecessary stress or undermine trust may not represent responsible organizational practices.

Notice that the warrant does more than repeat the evidence. It explains why the evidence matters and why it supports the recommendation.

You can use the three ethical perspectives to develop your reasoning:

  • Consequences: Explain why the outcomes of a decision make your recommendation appropriate.
  • Rights and Fairness: Explain why your recommendation respects people's rights and promotes fair treatment.
  • Character: Explain why your recommendation reflects ethical values and responsible leadership.

You do not have to use all three perspectives in every warrant. Choose the perspective or perspectives that best support your argument.

4. Rebuttal: Consider an Opposing Argument

A strong argument recognizes that reasonable people may disagree.

A rebuttal identifies an opposing position and explains why your original claim remains reasonable.

For example:

Opposing argument: Organizations need to monitor employees to protect confidential information and maintain productivity.

Response: Organizations do have legitimate reasons to monitor certain workplace activities. However, monitoring should be limited to appropriate business purposes and implemented transparently. This allows organizations to protect their operations while also respecting employee privacy and maintaining trust.

Ethical perspectives can help you evaluate opposing arguments. For example, the consequences perspective may help you recognize the organizational benefits of monitoring, while the rights and fairness perspective may help you explain why those benefits do not justify every form of monitoring.

Putting It All Together: An Organizational Example

Imagine you are a manager at a company considering a four-day workweek. Leadership wants to determine whether the organization should adopt the policy.

Your argument might look like this:

Claim

Organizations should consider implementing a four-day workweek when employees can maintain their responsibilities and organizational performance.

Evidence

Research indicates that shorter workweeks may improve employee well-being, reduce stress, and maintain or improve productivity under appropriate conditions.

Warrant

Organizations should consider workplace practices that support employee well-being while maintaining organizational effectiveness. From a consequences perspective, a four-day workweek may produce positive outcomes for both employees and the organization. From a rights and fairness perspective, the organization should also consider whether the policy can be implemented fairly across different positions and departments.

Rebuttal

Some managers may argue that a four-day workweek could reduce productivity, make customer service more difficult, or create scheduling problems. However, organizations can evaluate job responsibilities, customer needs, and performance outcomes before implementing the policy. A carefully designed policy may allow the organization to address these concerns while still providing employees with additional flexibility.

Notice how the ethical perspectives help explain why the recommendation is appropriate. They also help you recognize potential concerns and develop a reasonable response.

Final Takeaways

Ethical decision-making is an important part of organizational leadership. Managers and employees regularly make decisions that affect other people, workplace relationships, and organizational outcomes.

The three ethical perspectives provide a practical way to evaluate these decisions:

  • Consequences: Consider the benefits and potential harm of a decision.
  • Rights and Fairness: Consider whether people are respected and treated fairly.
  • Character: Consider whether the decision demonstrates honesty, integrity, and responsibility.

When developing an organizational argument, use these perspectives to move beyond personal opinion. Connect credible evidence to ethical reasoning, explain why your recommendation is appropriate, and consider reasonable opposing viewpoints.

The goal is not simply to argue for what an organization should do. The goal is to explain why the decision is supported by evidence and represents responsible organizational decision-making.
