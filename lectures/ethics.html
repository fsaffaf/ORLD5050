Ethics in Arguments

Overview

Organizations make ethical decisions every day. Ethics is both a way of thinking and a process of decision-making.

As an idea, ethics is a framework or perspective for understanding what is right and wrong. As an action, ethics involves thinking about what is right, wrong, fair, and responsible when making decisions.

Ethics and morals are closely related, but they are not exactly the same. Morals are an individual's beliefs about what is right and wrong. Ethics involves examining those beliefs, using principles or frameworks to evaluate decisions, and considering how actions affect others.

For example, someone may personally believe that employees should always work in the office because they value teamwork. However, ethical reasoning requires considering other factors, such as employee well-being, fairness, productivity, accessibility, and the organization's responsibilities before deciding whether an in-office policy is appropriate.

Ethical decisions in organizations involve considering how workplace policies, leadership choices, and business practices affect employees, customers, communities, and the organization itself.

Examples of ethical decisions include:

  • Should an organization monitor employees' computer activity?
  • Should employees be required to return to the office?
  • Should a company use artificial intelligence to make hiring decisions?
  • Should an organization prioritize employee satisfaction or cost reduction?
  • Should managers share information with employees when a major organizational change is being considered?

The answers to these questions are not always clear or obvious. Think about a time when a policy changed at your company or university. Did everyone agree with the change? Usually, people have different opinions, and it is rare for everyone to agree on what should be done.

A decision might benefit an organization financially but negatively affect employees. A policy might improve productivity but raise concerns about privacy or fairness. These situations can make it difficult to determine what the most ethical decision is.

Importantly, ethics is different from personal opinion. Personal opinions and morals reflect what an individual believes or considers right and wrong. Ethical perspectives provide ways to examine those beliefs, evaluate decisions, and explain why an action may be right, wrong, fair, or responsible.

The three ethical perspectives introduced in this course can be used to evaluate the same organizational decision. However, each perspective focuses on a different part of the ethical question, helping us understand why people may reach different conclusions about what should be done.

Three Main Ethical Perspectives

Ethical frameworks are ways of thinking about what is right, wrong, fair, or responsible. They come from philosophical ideas and debates developed over centuries about how people should behave, what they owe one another, and what makes a decision morally right. These ideas continue to influence how individuals, organizations, and societies evaluate decisions.

Ethical frameworks are different from an organization's ethical code. An ethical framework is a tool used to evaluate a decision and explain why it may be right or wrong. An organizational ethical code is a set of values, standards, and expectations established by an organization to guide behavior. It tells employees how they are expected to act within that organization.

For example, a company may have an ethical code that requires employees to respect others, protect confidential information, act honestly, and follow company policies. These expectations guide employees' behavior, but they do not necessarily answer whether every company policy or leadership decision is ethical.

Ethical frameworks allow us to examine those decisions more closely. We can use them to evaluate an organization's policies, question decisions, and explain whether an action is ethically appropriate, even when the organization permits or requires it.

Example: Returning to the Office

Imagine a company requires all employees to return to the office five days per week. Leadership believes working in the office will improve collaboration and productivity. However, some employees believe the policy negatively affects work-life balance, increases commuting costs, and reduces flexibility.

The company may have an ethical code that emphasizes respect, fairness, integrity, and responsibility. However, having those values does not automatically mean the return-to-office policy is ethical. The decision must still be evaluated.

An organizational ethical code might tell employees:

  • Follow workplace policies and expectations.
  • Treat coworkers respectfully.
  • Act honestly and responsibly.
  • Support the organization's goals.

Ethical frameworks help us ask different questions about the company's decision:

  • Consequences: Will requiring employees to return to the office produce more benefits than harm?
  • Justice: Does the policy respect employees' rights and treat them fairly?
  • Character: Does the decision demonstrate good character, such as honesty, empathy, fairness, and responsible leadership?

The company may expect employees to follow the policy, but employees and leaders can still use ethical frameworks to evaluate whether the decision is appropriate.

Three Main Ethical Perspectives

Ethical Perspective Official Name Main Focus Central Question
Consequences Consequentialism Outcomes and effects Does the outcome of this decision ultimately result in more benefits than harm?
Justice Deontology Rights, duties, responsibilities, and fair treatment Does this decision respect people's rights, responsibilities, and what is fair?

Sometimes these perspectives lead to the same decision, and sometimes they do not. Each perspective focuses on a different part of the ethical question. Using all three can help individuals evaluate a situation more completely, recognize competing concerns, and explain why a decision is ethically appropriate.

Remember: An organization can follow its own policies and ethical code and still make a decision that raises ethical concerns. Following organizational rules and making an ethical decision are not necessarily the same thing.

Consequences

Ethical Perspective Official Name Main Focus Central Question
Consequences Consequentialism Outcomes and effects Does the outcome of this decision ultimately result in more benefits than harm?

The consequences perspective focuses on the results of a decision. It asks whether a decision produces more benefits than harm for the people and groups affected.

When using this perspective, consider both the immediate and long-term outcomes. A decision might solve an immediate organizational problem but create additional problems over time.

Questions you should ask yourself:

  • Who benefits from this decision?
  • Who might be negatively affected?
  • Will the decision improve or harm employee well-being?
  • Will it improve organizational performance?
  • What are the possible short-term and long-term effects?
  • Do the overall benefits outweigh the potential harm?

Example: Employee Monitoring

Imagine an organization is considering monitoring employees' computer activity to improve productivity.

From a consequences perspective, you would consider the potential benefits and disadvantages. The ethical question is whether the expected benefits justify the potential negative effects.

Potential benefits:

  • Identifying inefficient work processes.
  • Improving information security.
  • Identifying employees who need additional support.
  • Increasing accountability.

Potential disadvantages:

  • Reducing employee trust.
  • Increasing workplace stress.
  • Making employees feel that they are constantly being watched.
  • Encouraging employees to focus on appearing busy rather than doing meaningful work.

How to Use This Perspective in an Argument

You might argue that an organization should limit employee monitoring because excessive monitoring can reduce employee trust and increase workplace stress. Research about employee satisfaction, workplace trust, and productivity could provide evidence to support this position.

Your reasoning would explain why an organization should consider the overall effects of its policies rather than focusing only on productivity.

Rights & Fairness

Ethical Perspective Official Name Main Focus Central Question
Justice Deontology Rights, duties, responsibilities, and fair treatment Does this decision respect people's rights, responsibilities, and what is fair?

The rights and fairness perspective focuses on whether decisions respect individuals and treat people fairly. It considers the responsibilities organizations have toward employees, customers, and other stakeholders.

People have legitimate interests and rights that organizations should consider when making decisions. These may include privacy, safety, equal treatment, respectful communication, and fair opportunities.

Fairness also means considering whether policies are applied consistently and whether individuals are treated appropriately. Treating everyone exactly the same is not always fair if people have different needs, circumstances, or barriers.

A decision is not necessarily ethical simply because it benefits the majority or improves organizational performance. Organizations must also consider whether the decision respects the people affected by it.

Questions to consider:

  • Does this decision respect people's rights?
  • Are people being treated fairly?
  • Are policies applied consistently?
  • Are employees given fair opportunities?
  • Are people being treated with dignity and respect?
  • Are individuals being excluded, disadvantaged, or treated unfairly?
  • Does the organization have a responsibility to protect the people affected by this decision?

Example: Artificial Intelligence in Hiring

Imagine an organization wants to use artificial intelligence to screen job applicants.

From a rights and fairness perspective, you would consider whether the technology treats applicants fairly.

For example:

  • Does the system evaluate applicants using job-related qualifications?
  • Could the system unfairly disadvantage certain applicants?
  • Are applicants informed about how their information is being used?
  • Can applicants question or challenge an unfair decision?
  • Does the organization provide a fair opportunity for qualified individuals?

Even if artificial intelligence saves time and reduces hiring costs, the organization must consider whether the process respects applicants and provides fair opportunities.

How to Use This Perspective in an Argument

You might argue that organizations should regularly evaluate artificial intelligence hiring systems to identify unfair outcomes.

Your evidence could include research about algorithmic bias, hiring practices, or employment discrimination. Your reasoning would explain why organizations have a responsibility to use hiring practices that respect applicants and provide fair opportunities.

Character

Ethical Perspective Official Name Main Focus Central Question
Character Virtue Ethics Values, character, and ethical leadership Does this decision demonstrate good character, such as honesty, integrity, and trustworthiness?

The character perspective focuses on the values and qualities demonstrated through a person's or organization's decisions.

It asks what an ethical person, manager, or organization would do. Rather than focusing only on outcomes or rules, this perspective considers the character behind a decision.

Important ethical qualities include:

  • Honesty: Communicating truthfully and avoiding deception.
  • Integrity: Doing what is right, even when it is difficult or inconvenient.
  • Responsibility: Accepting accountability for decisions and their effects.
  • Respect: Treating others with dignity and consideration.
  • Transparency: Being open about decisions, policies, and relevant information.
  • Trustworthiness: Acting in ways that others can depend on.

In organizations, ethical character is demonstrated through leadership decisions, communication, workplace policies, and everyday interactions.

Questions to consider:

  • Is the decision honest and transparent?
  • Does it demonstrate integrity?
  • Is the organization accepting responsibility for its actions?
  • Would an ethical leader make this decision?
  • Does the decision demonstrate respect for employees and customers?
  • Does the organization follow its stated values?
  • Would the organization be comfortable explaining this decision publicly?

Organizational Example: Communicating Organizational Changes

Imagine an organization plans to eliminate several positions because of financial difficulties. Leadership knows that employees will be affected but delays communicating the decision to avoid uncomfortable conversations.

From a character perspective, you would consider whether leadership is demonstrating honesty, integrity, and responsibility.

An ethical approach would involve communicating relevant information honestly, treating affected employees respectfully, and accepting responsibility for the organization's decisions.

Even when a decision is financially necessary, the way leadership communicates and handles the situation matters.

How to Use This Perspective in an Argument

You might argue that organizations should communicate major workplace changes transparently, even when the information is difficult to share.

Your evidence could include research about organizational trust, employee communication, and leadership. Your reasoning would explain why honesty and responsibility are important qualities of ethical leadership.

How Ethical Perspectives Can Shape an Argument

Ethical perspectives can influence how an organizational decision is evaluated and how an argument is developed. The same ethical perspective can be used to support a main claim or challenge it, depending on how the decision is evaluated. The examples below use the same main claim to demonstrate how different ethical perspectives can lead to different arguments.

Example Main Claim

Organizations should allow employees to work remotely when their jobs can be performed effectively outside the office.

Using Ethical Perspectives to Support a Subclaim

A subclaim identifies a specific reason supporting the main claim. Each ethical perspective provides a different reason why an organization should allow remote work.

Ethical Perspective Example Subclaim
Consequences Organizations should allow remote work because it can improve employee well-being, reduce commuting stress, and increase job satisfaction.
Rights and Fairness Organizations should allow remote work when possible because employees should have fair opportunities to work under conditions that support their needs, as long as job responsibilities are fulfilled.
Character Organizations should allow remote work because trusting employees to manage their responsibilities demonstrates respect, integrity, and responsible leadership.

Using Ethical Perspectives to Develop a Counterargument

A counterargument presents a reasonable opposing position. Each ethical perspective can also be used to explain why someone might disagree with the main claim and support requiring employees to work in the office.

Ethical Perspective Example Counterargument
Consequences Organizations should require employees to work in the office when necessary because in-person collaboration may improve communication, teamwork, and overall organizational performance.
Rights and Fairness Organizations should be able to establish consistent workplace expectations for employees because employers have responsibilities to manage operations, coordinate work, and ensure employees meet their job requirements.
Character Organizations should encourage employees to work in the office because being physically present can demonstrate commitment to coworkers, teamwork, and shared organizational goals.

These examples demonstrate how ethical perspectives can be used to support different positions on the same organizational decision. The perspective does not automatically determine whether an argument supports or opposes the main claim. Instead, the writer uses ethical reasoning to develop a position, explain why it is important, and support it with evidence.
