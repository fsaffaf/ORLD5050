Argument Structure

Overview

Constructing a clear and well-supported argument is the main goal of Ethical, Evidence-Based Decision Making (EEBDM). In the professional world, individuals should be able to justify their decisions by providing reasons and evidence. If you understand how to create a solid argument, you can communicate your ideas effectively and assess the reasoning of others.

In everyday language, the word "argument" often refers to a disagreement or conflict. In academic and professional contexts, however, an argument has a more precise meaning.

Argument: In the professional world, an argument is an organized set of reasons that provide support for a specific decision.

  • Helps you think critically and logically.
  • Improves clarity in writing and presentations.
  • Supports stronger, evidence-based decisions.
  • Allows you to evaluate others' reasoning.

Below is a simple argument map. The main claim is supported by three subclaims. Each subclaim is supported by three premises (reasons). Each reason is supported by one piece of evidence. There are also three counterarguments with a rebuttal against each counterargument. In the real world, you would likely have more than three subclaims, more than three premises, and definitely more than one piece of evidence to support each premise.
