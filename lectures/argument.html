Argument Structure

Lecture 3

Overview

Constructing a clear and well-supported argument is a central component of Ethical, Evidence-Based Decision Making (EEBDM). Whether in academic, professional, or everyday situations, individuals must often justify their decisions by providing reasons and evidence. Understanding how arguments are structured and how they can be evaluated allows decision makers to communicate their reasoning effectively and assess the reasoning of others.

In everyday language, the word "argument" often refers to a disagreement or conflict. In academic and professional contexts, however, an argument has a more precise meaning.

Argument: In the professional world, an argument is an organized set of reasons that provide support for a specific decision.

Basic Argument

Basic Argument: A specific type of argument made up of two parts: the claim (or conclusion) and the premises.
Component Definition Example Statement
Claim The main point someone is trying to prove or convince others to accept. Single-use plastics should be banned.
Premise The reasons that support the claim and justify why it should be accepted. Single-use plastics cause significant environmental harm.
Evidence Support used to back up premises. Plastic waste pollutes oceans and harms marine life.

Complex Argument (Toulmin Model)

Complex Argument: Complex arguments expand on basic arguments by including additional components such as warrants, backing, qualifiers, counterarguments, and rebuttals.

Understanding Toulmin Arguments

Component What It Means (Simple) Key Question Example
Claim The main point you are arguing for. What do I want people to believe? I believe that ... single-use plastics should be banned.
Premise (Reason) The reason supporting your claim. Why is my claim true? This is because ... they cause serious environmental harm.
Evidence Facts or data that support your reason. What proof do I have? Evidence shows that ... plastic waste harms ocean ecosystems.
Warrant The logic connecting your reason to your claim. Why does this reason support the claim? If ... plastic waste harms ocean ecosystems, then ... those products should be restricted.
Backing Support for the warrant. Why should we accept this logic? Additional research shows ... plastic pollution is widespread.
Qualifier Limits how strong or broad your claim is. How certain or broad is my claim? A limitation to my claim is that ... there are some cases where there is no alternative to plastic.
Counterargument An opposing viewpoint. What might others disagree with? Some may argue that ... certain industries depend on plastics.
Rebuttal Your response to the counterargument. How do I respond to objections? However, I would argue that ... exceptions and alternatives can address this.

Putting It Together

Example Argument

Single-use plastics should be banned (claim) because they cause significant environmental harm (premise), supported by evidence that plastic waste pollutes oceans and harms marine life (evidence). Since harmful products should be regulated when alternatives exist (warrant), and research supports both the harm and effectiveness of bans (backing), this policy is justified in most contexts where alternatives are accessible (qualifier). Some industries depend on single-use plastics because they are cheap, safe, and widely available, so a ban could create practical problems (counterargument). These concerns can be addressed through limited exceptions and gradual changes, while alternatives become more affordable and accessible over time (rebuttal).

Why This Matters

  • Helps you think critically and logically.
  • Improves clarity in writing and presentations.
  • Supports stronger, evidence-based decisions.
  • Allows you to evaluate others’ reasoning.

Key Takeaways

  • Arguments organize reasoning and decision-making.
  • Basic arguments include claims and premises.
  • Complex arguments add depth through warrants, evidence, and counterarguments.
  • Strong arguments rely on clear, relevant, and well-supported evidence.
