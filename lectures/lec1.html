What is Ethical Evidence-Based Decision-Making?

EEBDM

Overview

Organizations make decisions every day. These decisions affect employees, customers, investors, and communities. They can also affect whether an organization succeeds in the future.

Different people in an organization make different kinds of decisions. Leaders decide what goals to focus on. Managers decide how to use time and resources. Human resource professionals make hiring decisions. Healthcare and public administrators make policy decisions.

Some decisions are routine, such as creating work schedules or choosing a supplier. Other decisions are much bigger and can affect an organization for many years. Examples include launching a new product, entering a new market, changing a department, using new technology, or responding to a crisis.

Because decisions are so important, organizations need a good way to make them.

However, people do not always use a clear process when making decisions. They may rely on habits, traditions, personal experience, or limited information. Sometimes these approaches work. Other times, they can lead to mistakes, unfair decisions, or unnecessary risks.

Organizations today face many changes. New technology, changing customer needs, competition, and different stakeholder concerns can make decisions more difficult. Decision-makers need more than a gut feeling or a simple rule. They need a way to gather information, compare choices, explain their decisions, and learn from the results.

Ethical, Evidence-Based Decision-Making (EEBDM) provides this type of approach. EEBDM combines decisions, evidence, and ethics to help people make choices that are both effective and responsible.

The goal is not to find a perfect answer. A perfect answer may not exist. Instead, EEBDM helps decision-makers make the best choice they can based on the information they have and the situation they are facing.

The Three Parts of EEBDM

Part Question
Decisions What are our choices?
Evidence What information can help us choose?
Ethics What choice is right, fair, and responsible?

By using evidence and ethics together, organizations can make better decisions, improve their work, build trust, and treat people fairly.

The EEBDM Process

EEBDM is more than a list of ideas. It is a process that can be used to work through a real decision.

  1. Identify the Decision
    What decision needs to be made?
  2. Identify the Options
    What could we do?
  3. Identify the Stakeholders
    Who could be affected?
  4. Gather and Evaluate Evidence
    What do we know, and can we trust it?
  5. Consider the Ethical Issues
    What is right, fair, and responsible?
  6. Compare the Options and Decide
    Which option best meets our goals and responsibilities?
  7. Act, Evaluate, and Learn
    What happened, and what can we learn?
EEBDM Process:

Identify the Decision   →   Identify the Options   →   Identify Stakeholders   →   Evaluate Evidence   →   Consider Ethics   →   Decide   →   Learn

Decisions

An organizational decision means choosing what to do from two or more possible choices. Every decision can have consequences.

For example, a decision may affect:

  • Money and profits
  • Employees
  • Customers
  • Legal requirements
  • The organization's reputation
  • The organization's future

Some decisions happen often and are part of everyday work. These might include managing supplies or creating employee schedules.

Other decisions are much larger. They may involve major changes, large investments, mergers, acquisitions, or new policies.

In real life, decision-makers usually cannot look at every possible choice or collect every piece of information. They often have limited time, information, and resources.

Satisficing Decisions

Satisficing means choosing an option that meets the most important needs, even if it is not perfect.

Satisficing:
A choice that is not perfect, but is "good enough" for the situation.

Organizations often do not have enough time or money to carefully study every possible choice. Leaders may also need to make a decision before they have all the information.

For example, imagine that a human resources director needs to choose a new computer system for hiring employees. There may be dozens of systems to choose from. Looking at every system in great detail would take a lot of time and money.

Instead, the director might decide what features are most important, such as:

  • Cost
  • Security
  • Features
  • Ability to work with other systems
  • Ease of use

The director can then choose a system that meets the most important needs.

Satisficing does not mean making a bad decision. It is a practical way to make decisions when time, information, or resources are limited.

Stakeholders

Stakeholders are people or groups who are affected by an organization's decisions.

Stakeholders may include:

  • Employees
  • Customers
  • Managers
  • Investors
  • Suppliers
  • Communities
  • Other groups

Identifying stakeholders helps decision-makers understand who may be helped, harmed, or otherwise affected by each option.

Evidence

Evidence is information that helps people make decisions. It can help decision-makers compare choices, understand possible results, identify risks, and explain why they made a decision.

Evidence:
Information used to help guide a decision. Good evidence should be relevant, trustworthy, and appropriate for the decision.

Evidence can include:

  • Organizational data
  • Financial reports
  • Market research
  • Industry information
  • Academic research
  • Professional knowledge
  • Feedback from stakeholders
  • Work performance data
  • Predictions and forecasts
  • Past experiences

However, not all information is good evidence.

Organizations often have more information than they know what to do with. The challenge is deciding which information is actually useful.

Information becomes useful evidence when it is relevant to the decision and comes from a source we can trust.

Decision-makers should ask:

  • Is this information related to the decision?
  • Can I trust this information?
  • Is the information accurate?
  • Does this information apply to this situation?

The goal is not to collect as much information as possible. The goal is to use the right information to help make the decision.

Ethics

Organizations affect many people. Because organizational decisions can affect others, decision-makers need to think about more than business goals. They also need to think about what is right, fair, and responsible.

Ethics:
Ways of thinking about what is right, fair, and responsible.
Evidence Ethics
What is likely to happen? What should we do?

Evidence can help us understand what might happen if we make a certain choice. Ethics helps us think about whether that choice is right and responsible.

A decision can work well for an organization and still be unethical.

For example, an organization might make more money by cutting employee pay. The decision could save money, but it could also create problems for employees.

EEBDM asks decision-makers to think about both results and responsibility.

Ethics Before and After Decisions

Before a Decision

Before making a decision, ethical thinking can help decision-makers identify choices that should not be used, even if those choices could provide benefits.

For example, an option might save money but treat people unfairly. Ethics can help decision-makers recognize this problem before making the choice.

After a Decision

Ethics can also be used after a decision is made.

Organizations can ask:

  • Did the decision match our values?
  • Did the decision treat people fairly?
  • Did anything unexpected happen?
  • Did the decision cause harm?
  • What can we learn from the experience?

This helps organizations learn from their decisions and improve in the future.

Ethical Dilemmas

An ethical dilemma happens when two or more important values conflict.

Ethical dilemmas are common in organizations. For example, an organization may have to balance:

  • Profit and employee well-being
  • Transparency and confidentiality
  • Efficiency and fairness
  • Innovation and privacy

These situations can be difficult because there may not be an obvious right answer.

Decision-makers must look carefully at their choices and think about how each choice could affect different stakeholders.

Ethical decision-making models, such as the Santa Clara University ethical decision-making model, can help people look at a problem from different points of view.

A good decision considers both the results of the decision and how the decision affects other people.

Key Takeaways

  • Decision-making is a process, not a random choice.
  • Satisficing means choosing an option that meets the most important needs when time, information, or resources are limited.
  • Evidence helps us understand what is likely to happen.
  • Good evidence is relevant, trustworthy, and appropriate for the decision.
  • Ethics helps us think about what is right, fair, and responsible.
  • Ethical dilemmas happen when important values conflict.
  • EEBDM combines decisions, evidence, and ethics to support better decisions.
  • The EEBDM process helps decision-makers identify the decision, examine options, consider stakeholders, evaluate evidence, consider ethics, make a decision, and learn from the results.
