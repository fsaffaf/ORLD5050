Understanding Warrants

Overview

A warrant is the reasoning that explains why a premise supports a subclaim. It connects the specific point you are making (premise) to the larger reason you are trying to establish (subclaim).

In an argument, it is not enough to provide a premise and assume your audience will understand why it matters. You must explain the connection between the premise and your subclaim. The warrant makes that connection clear.

Main claim: The overall position you want your audience to accept.
Subclaims: The major reasons supporting your main claim.
Premises: The specific statements that provide reasons to accept each subclaim.
Warrants: The reasoning that explains why each premise supports its subclaim.

Think of a warrant as the explanation behind your reasoning. It helps your audience understand why the information you have presented is relevant and how it contributes to your argument.

How Does a Warrant Work?

A warrant connects a premise to its corresponding subclaim. It explains why the premise provides a logical reason to accept the subclaim.

Example Argument

Main Claim: Organizations should implement a four-day workweek.
Subclaim: A four-day workweek can improve employee well-being.

Example Premise

Providing employees with additional time away from work can help them manage personal responsibilities and maintain a healthier work-life balance.

Example Warrant

Employees who have sufficient time to manage their personal responsibilities and recover from work-related demands are more likely to experience better overall well-being.

The premise identifies a specific benefit of additional time away from work. The warrant explains why that benefit matters to employee well-being. Together, they establish the reasoning behind the subclaim.

What Does a Warrant Actually Explain?

A warrant answers the question: "Why does this premise provide a good reason to believe my subclaim?"

When developing a warrant, you are explaining the relationship between two ideas. You are showing your audience why the specific point identified in your premise contributes to the larger point identified in your subclaim.

Questions to Help Develop a Warrant

  • Why does this premise matter to my subclaim?
  • How does the point identified in my premise contribute to the outcome described in my subclaim?
  • What relationship exists between the premise and the subclaim?
  • What would my audience need to understand to see why this premise supports my subclaim?
Useful Test: If a reader asks, "So what? Why does that matter?", your warrant should provide the explanation.

How Do You Develop a Warrant?

Warrants are developed by thinking critically about the relationship between your premises and subclaims. You are not simply repeating information from your research. You are explaining the reasoning that connects the two.

Step 1: Identify Your Premise

Start with one premise that you have developed to support a particular subclaim. Make sure you understand the specific point the premise is making.

Step 2: Identify Your Subclaim

Review the subclaim that the premise is intended to support. Identify the larger reason or outcome that you are trying to establish.

Step 3: Explain the Connection

Ask yourself why the premise provides a reason to accept the subclaim. Think about the relationship between the two statements. Consider the underlying principle, process, or relationship that makes the premise relevant.

Step 4: Write Your Warrant

Write a statement that clearly explains the connection between the premise and the subclaim. Your warrant should add reasoning rather than simply restating either statement.

Step 5: Check the Logic

Read your premise, warrant, and subclaim together. Ask whether the warrant actually explains why the premise supports the subclaim. If the connection is unclear, revise your reasoning.

Distinguish Between a Premise and a Warrant

Premises and warrants are closely related, but they serve different purposes. A premise identifies a specific reason supporting a subclaim. A warrant explains why that reason is relevant to the subclaim.

Component Purpose
Subclaim Identifies a major reason supporting your main claim.
Premise Identifies a specific point that helps establish the subclaim.
Warrant Explains why the premise provides a logical reason to accept the subclaim.
Evidence Provides research findings, facts, statistics, or expert information that supports the premise.
Important: A warrant is not another piece of evidence. It is the reasoning that explains the connection between your premise and subclaim.

Do Warrants Need Evidence?

Warrants are based on reasoning, but they should still be logical and defensible. Some warrants rely on widely accepted principles or relationships. Others may involve assumptions that your audience does not automatically accept.

If your warrant depends on an assumption that is questionable or difficult to establish, you may need additional research to support that reasoning. For example, an audience may not automatically agree that a particular organizational change will produce a specific employee or business outcome.

Consider whether your warrant is reasonable, whether it relies on an unsupported assumption, and whether your audience would need additional information to accept the connection.

Final Considerations

Developing warrants requires you to explain the logic behind your argument. Your premises identify the specific points you want to establish. Your warrants explain why those points matter to your subclaims.

Before finalizing a warrant, ask yourself:

  • Does my warrant explain why the premise supports the subclaim?
  • Does it explain the relationship between the two statements?
  • Does it add reasoning rather than repeat the premise?
  • Is the reasoning logical and defensible?
  • Would my audience understand why the premise matters after reading the warrant?
