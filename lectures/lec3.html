Argument Structure

Lecture 3

Overview

Constructing a clear and well-supported argument is a central component of Ethical, Evidence-Based Decision Making (EEBDM). Whether in academic, professional, or everyday situations, individuals must often justify their decisions by providing reasons and evidence. Understanding how arguments are structured and how they can be evaluated allows decision makers to communicate their reasoning effectively and assess the reasoning of others.

In everyday language, the word "argument" often refers to a disagreement or conflict. In academic and professional contexts, however, an argument has a more precise meaning.

Argument

In the professional world, an argument is an organized set of reasons that provide support for a specific decision.

Basic Argument

Basic Argument

A specific type of argument made up of two parts: the claim (or conclusion) and the premises.

Component Definition Example Statement
Claim The main point someone is trying to prove or convince others to accept. Single-use plastics should be banned.
Premise The reasons that support the claim and justify why it should be accepted. Single-use plastics cause significant environmental harm.
Evidence Support used to back up premises. Plastic waste pollutes oceans and harms marine life.

Complex Argument (Toulmin Model)

Complex Argument

Complex arguments expand on basic arguments by including additional components such as warrants, backing, qualifiers, counterarguments, and rebuttals.

Component Definition Example Statement
Claim The main point being argued. Single-use plastics should be banned.
Premise Reason explaining how evidence supports the claim. Single-use plastics cause significant environmental harm.
Evidence Data supporting the premise. Plastic waste pollutes oceans and harms marine life.
Warrant Connects the premise to the claim. Products that cause substantial, long-term environmental harm should be regulated or banned when safer alternatives exist.
Backing Additional support for the warrant (often includes ethical or empirical support). Scientific research shows plastics are a major source of ocean pollution. Countries like France and Kenya have reduced waste through bans.
Qualifier Indicates the strength or scope of the claim. Single-use plastics should be banned in most cases where alternatives are accessible.
Counterargument An opposing viewpoint. Bans may create challenges in sectors like healthcare or food safety.
Rebuttal Response defending the original claim. Targeted exemptions and gradual implementation can address these concerns while reducing overall waste.

Putting It Together

Example Argument

Single-use plastics should be banned (claim) because they cause significant environmental harm (premise), supported by evidence that plastic waste pollutes oceans and harms marine life (evidence). Since harmful products should be regulated when alternatives exist (warrant), and research supports both the harm and effectiveness of bans (backing), this policy is justified in most contexts where alternatives are accessible (qualifier).

Why This Matters

  • Helps you think critically and logically.
  • Improves clarity in writing and presentations.
  • Supports stronger, evidence-based decisions.
  • Allows you to evaluate others’ reasoning.

Key Takeaways

  • Arguments organize reasoning and decision-making.
  • Basic arguments include claims and premises.
  • Complex arguments add depth through warrants, evidence, and counterarguments.
  • Strong arguments rely on clear, relevant, and well-supported evidence.
