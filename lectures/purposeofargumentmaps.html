Purpose of Argument Maps

Overview

An argument map is a visual representation of an argument. It organizes the different parts of an argument so you can see how they connect and support one another. Instead of thinking about an argument as one large collection of ideas, an argument map breaks it into smaller pieces and shows how each piece contributes to the overall position.

An argument map typically includes a main claim, subclaims, premises, evidence, warrants, counterarguments, and rebuttals. Each component has a specific purpose, and the map shows how they work together to build and defend an argument.

Argument Map: A visual tool that organizes the parts of an argument and shows how reasons and evidence support a main claim.

An important thing to understand is that an argument map is not just a diagram you create for an assignment. It is also a mental tool that provides a framework for how you think about arguments. Once you understand how arguments are structured, you can use this framework when developing your own arguments, reading research articles, evaluating professional recommendations, or having conversations with other people.

You do not always need to draw an actual map to use this approach. You can mentally picture the different parts of an argument and consider how they connect.

Why Are Argument Maps Useful?

Argument maps are useful because they help you understand the structure of an argument rather than simply focusing on what the argument says. They allow you to step back, look at the bigger picture, and examine how the different parts connect.

1. Break Down Your Own Argument

When developing an argument, it can be difficult to keep track of all your ideas, reasons, and supporting evidence. An argument map helps you mentally break down your argument into smaller, more manageable pieces.

Instead of thinking about your entire argument at once, you can focus on individual parts:

  • What is my main claim?
  • What are the different reasons supporting my main claim?
  • What facts support each reason?
  • What evidence shows that those facts are accurate?
  • Why does the evidence support my reasoning?
  • What opposing viewpoints should I address?

Breaking your argument into these parts makes it easier to organize your thinking. It also helps you see whether all your reasons work together to support your overall position.

Over time, you can begin to visualize this structure mentally whenever you are developing an argument. Rather than simply writing down everything you think about a topic, you can organize your thoughts by considering where each idea belongs and how it contributes to your overall position.

2. Organize Your Research and Supporting Materials

An argument map is especially useful while you are developing your argument and collecting research articles. Rather than collecting articles and trying to figure out how they fit into your argument later, you can use your argument map as an organizational tool throughout the research process.

Think of your argument map as a blueprint for your research. It shows you what information you need to find and where that information belongs.

For example, as you collect research articles, you can identify which subclaim, premise, or part of your argument each article supports. This allows you to organize your research according to the purpose it serves rather than simply keeping a collection of unrelated articles.

Organizing your materials this way helps you:

  • Identify what research you need: Your argument map shows which premises need supporting facts and evidence. You can use this information to guide your searches for research articles.
  • Keep research organized: You can identify which subclaim and premise each article supports, making it easier to locate and use information when writing your paper.
  • Avoid collecting unrelated research: An article may be interesting or relevant to your general topic without actually supporting your argument. Your map helps you evaluate whether the information contributes to one of your specific points.
  • Identify research gaps: You may discover that you have plenty of research supporting one subclaim but very little supporting another. This helps you recognize where additional research is needed.
  • See how your research works together: You can examine whether your collected evidence provides enough support for your premises and whether your premises adequately support your subclaims.

This process also prevents you from having to reorganize a large collection of research materials at the end of your project. Instead, you are building and organizing your argument as you conduct your research.

Remember: Your argument map should develop as your argument develops. You may add new premises, find additional evidence, revise subclaims, or change your position as you learn more about your topic. Your map is a working document that helps guide this process.

3. See How the Parts of Your Argument Connect

An argument is not simply a collection of opinions, facts, and research articles. The different parts must connect logically.

An argument map helps you see these connections. For example, each subclaim should provide a reason to support the main claim. Each premise should provide a reason to support its subclaim, and evidence should demonstrate that the premise is accurate. A warrant explains why the evidence supports the reasoning being presented.

When these connections are visible, you can evaluate whether your argument makes sense as a whole.

You may discover that:

  • A subclaim does not actually support your main claim.
  • A premise is unrelated to the subclaim it is supposed to support.
  • You have evidence, but you have not explained what it demonstrates.
  • Your reasoning is missing an important connection.

Identifying these problems early allows you to strengthen your argument before writing your final paper or presenting your position.

4. Analyze Written Arguments and Research Articles

Argument maps are also useful when reading and evaluating information written by other people.

When you read an article, report, or professional recommendation, you can use the structure of an argument map to mentally dissect what the author is communicating. Rather than simply reading the information and deciding whether you agree, you can break down the author's reasoning.

Ask yourself:

  • What is the author's main claim?
  • What reasons are they providing to support that claim?
  • What facts and evidence are they using?
  • How does the evidence connect to their reasoning?
  • What assumptions are they making?
  • Are there opposing viewpoints they have not considered?

This approach helps you distinguish between what an author is claiming, why they believe it, and whether they have provided enough support for their position.

You can also identify weaknesses in an article, such as unsupported statements, insufficient evidence, or reasoning that does not logically connect to the main claim.

Being able to mentally map an argument makes you a more critical reader because you are evaluating how the author reached their conclusion rather than simply accepting the information presented.

5. Understand and Evaluate Arguments During Conversations

Argument maps are not limited to written assignments or research. You can also use them in everyday conversations and professional discussions.

Imagine that you are having a conversation with a coworker who disagrees with a recommendation you have made. Instead of treating the disagreement as one large disagreement about the entire topic, you can break down the conversation into the different parts of your argument.

For example, you can ask yourself:

  • Does this person disagree with my main claim?
  • Do they disagree with one of my reasons (subclaims)?
  • Do they question the facts I am using to support my position?
  • Do they disagree with how I am interpreting the evidence?
  • Are they making an assumption that I have not considered?

This is important because two people may disagree about a decision for completely different reasons. One person might agree with your overall goal but disagree with the evidence supporting your recommendation. Another person might agree with your evidence but disagree with the conclusion you have drawn from it.

Understanding exactly where the disagreement occurs helps you respond more effectively. Rather than repeating your entire argument, you can address the specific point of disagreement.

The same approach works when you are trying to explain your own position. If someone does not understand your argument, mentally mapping your reasoning can help you identify which connection you have not explained clearly. Perhaps you have provided evidence but have not explained why it supports your position. Perhaps you have not clearly communicated how one of your reasons connects to your main claim.

In these situations, an argument map helps you recognize where communication is breaking down so you can clarify your reasoning.

6. Identify Counterarguments and Develop Rebuttals

One of the most important benefits of an argument map is that it helps you identify opposing viewpoints and develop responses.

When developing an argument, it is easy to focus only on information that supports your own position. However, strong arguments also consider why someone might disagree.

A counterargument represents an opposing viewpoint. A rebuttal responds to that opposing viewpoint and explains why your original position remains reasonable.

An argument map makes it easier to identify where someone might challenge your reasoning. An opposing viewpoint might challenge your main claim, question one of your subclaims, disagree with a premise, or point out a weakness in your evidence.

You can use this same approach when someone presents an argument that you disagree with. Instead of simply disagreeing with their conclusion, you can examine the specific reasons and evidence they have provided and identify where their reasoning may be weak.

This allows you to:

  • Identify the strongest opposing viewpoints.
  • Recognize unsupported assumptions.
  • Identify weaknesses in someone else's evidence or reasoning.
  • Develop responses that address the actual disagreement.
  • Strengthen your own position by considering alternative perspectives.

The goal is not simply to prove someone else wrong. The goal is to understand the reasoning behind different positions and evaluate whether the supporting information is convincing.

Argument Maps and Professional Decision-Making

Argument maps are useful beyond academic assignments. They can help you evaluate decisions and communicate recommendations in the workplace.

Consider how often professionals must explain why a particular decision should be made. A manager might recommend changing a company policy, an analyst might recommend a new technology, or a department might propose a different approach to completing its work.

In each situation, the person making the recommendation should be able to explain:

  • What decision they are recommending.
  • Why they believe the decision is appropriate.
  • What facts and evidence support their recommendation.
  • How the evidence connects to their reasoning.
  • What concerns or opposing viewpoints should be considered.

An argument map helps organize this information so that the decision-maker can examine the reasoning before presenting a recommendation.

It also helps other people evaluate the recommendation. Instead of focusing only on whether they agree with the final decision, they can examine the reasons and evidence supporting it.

Final Takeaway

An argument map is more than a diagram. It is a visual and mental framework for organizing, understanding, and evaluating arguments.

When creating your own argument, it helps you organize your ideas and research, identify missing information, connect evidence to reasoning, and prepare for opposing viewpoints. When reading someone else's work, it helps you break down their position and evaluate the quality of their reasoning. When having conversations, it helps you identify exactly where disagreements occur, clarify misunderstandings, and develop responses to opposing viewpoints.

You can use the structure of an argument map even when you are not physically drawing one. The more familiar you become with the different components of an argument, the easier it becomes to recognize and use this structure in your academic work, professional decisions, and everyday conversations.

Ultimately, the purpose of an argument map is to help you think more clearly about both what someone is arguing and why their argument should be considered reasonable.
