Heuristics & Biases

Overview

  • Intuitive and analytical thinking work together during decision making.
  • Heuristics are mental shortcuts that help us make decisions efficiently.
  • Heuristics can be useful, but they can also produce inaccurate decisions when important information is overlooked.
  • Biases are often outside conscious awareness and can influence how we interpret information.
  • Biases can sometimes support efficient judgment, but they can also contribute to errors, groupthink, and resistance to new evidence.
  • Effective decision making involves recognizing when shortcuts are useful and when more careful analysis is needed.
In EEBDM, the goal is not to eliminate shortcuts. The goal is to recognize them, evaluate them, and know when to slow down.

Intuitive & Analytical Thinking

Decision making involves two closely connected types of thinking: intuitive thinking and analytical thinking.

Intuitive Thinking
A fast, automatic form of thinking based on patterns, experience, and immediate impressions.
Analytical Thinking
A slower, more deliberate way of thinking that evaluates information, evidence, and outcomes.

Approaches to decision making can also vary across cultures and contexts. Some cultures and institutions place greater emphasis on explicit analytical reasoning, while others may place greater value on intuition, experience, relationships, and contextual judgment. In practice, both forms of thinking can contribute to decisions.

Intuitive and analytical thinking should not be viewed as completely separate processes. These two methods of thinking can work together during decision making.

For example, when searching for a house, intuition may draw your attention to certain listings that feel appealing, while analytical reasoning helps you review details such as price, inspections, and long-term suitability. Intuition helps narrow options quickly, while analytical thinking helps evaluate them more thoroughly.

Effective decision making comes from balancing intuitive judgments with careful analysis.

Heuristics

The human brain cannot process every piece of information available, all the time, every day. If we tried to carefully analyze every detail, it would take too long to make even simple decisions, such as when to wake up, what to eat, what to wear, or how to get to class.

Therefore, the human brain often uses mental shortcuts called heuristics.

Heuristics
Intentional mental shortcuts that simplify decision making by focusing on key information.

Heuristics allow us to make faster decisions by focusing on a limited amount of information rather than carefully analyzing every possible detail. Decisions made using heuristics can be accurate or inaccurate.

Benefits

These shortcuts allow us to make practical decisions in complex situations. They are widely used in everyday life and help us function efficiently in environments where time, information, and cognitive resources are limited.

For example, if a decision we made yesterday worked successfully, such as deciding when to leave the house to arrive at class on time, it may be reasonable to make the same choice today.

However, it is not guaranteed that decisions made using heuristics will always be accurate.

Limitations

Heuristics focus on only a small portion of the available information. If important details are ignored, the decision may be flawed.

For example, choosing a job candidate based only on GPA may overlook important qualities such as work experience.

Additionally, heuristics typically work best in environments where we have relevant experience. When situations are new or unfamiliar, the shortcut may no longer be reliable.

For example, brand recognition may help when buying common products but may fail when choosing items in a completely new category.

Solutions

Our brains will use heuristics regularly. Therefore, in EEBDM, we analyze our heuristics to determine when they are useful and lead to accurate decisions versus when they are not useful and lead to inaccurate decisions.

To analyze our heuristics, we must become more attentive to the criteria driving our decisions: what we focus on, what we overlook, and why.

When decision criteria are thoughtfully chosen, even simplified decisions can be well-reasoned decisions.

Heuristics help people make fast, efficient, and often effective decisions, but because they rely on simplified information, they can sometimes produce biases or mistakes.

Effective decision making involves recognizing when heuristics are useful and when more careful analysis is needed.

Types of Heuristics

Heuristic Definition Helpful Harmful
Well-Known Heuristic Choose what you recognize. Quick choice of familiar products. Influenced by advertising rather than quality.
Popularity Heuristic Follow what others choose. Works when the group is informed. Fails when driven by trends.
Similarity Heuristic Use past similar experiences. Effective when situations match. Misleading if situations differ.
Auto-Pilot Heuristic Accept the default option. Efficient when defaults are good. Risky if defaults are harmful.
Emotion Heuristic Decide based on feelings. Useful when emotions signal danger. Can lead to impulsive actions.
Recent Heuristic Focus on recent events. Helpful when trends are real. Misleading if events are rare.

Biases

Biases are closely related to heuristics. Heuristics can involve shortcuts that we recognize we are using, while biases often operate outside of our conscious awareness.

One way to think about biases is as intuitive shortcuts. Biases influence how we interpret information and make decisions, often without us realizing that they are affecting our judgment.

Biases
Unintentional shortcuts that simplify decisions but may introduce error.

Biases can operate outside conscious awareness and influence how we interpret information. Like heuristics, they can help us reach decisions quickly when time, information, or cognitive resources are limited.

The brain may focus on certain information and give less attention to other details. As a result, decisions influenced by biases can sometimes be accurate and sometimes be inaccurate.

Benefits

Biases can provide practical advantages by helping individuals and groups make faster, more efficient decisions.

For example, filtering and prioritizing information can focus attention on key aspects when someone is overwhelmed. A doctor, for instance, may concentrate first on the most likely diagnosis rather than immediately investigating every rare possibility.

Anchoring decisions in core beliefs can also provide consistency and stability by helping people make choices that align with long-held values.

Value-driven choices may simplify decisions by emphasizing ethical or moral priorities. For example, a nonprofit leader might favor projects that most closely align with the organization's mission.

Biases may also facilitate decisions under uncertainty by directing attention toward particular cues, such as an emergency responder prioritizing victims showing visible distress.

Finally, processes that reinforce identity and social cohesion can promote harmony within groups, while reducing cognitive dissonance can help individuals maintain a coherent understanding of their past choices.

Limitations

While these processes can be adaptive, they can also create significant problems. Filtering and prioritizing information can cause critical evidence to be overlooked, resulting in one-sided decisions.

Anchoring in core beliefs may block necessary change, causing stagnation or missed opportunities.

Value-driven simplification can prevent flexible thinking and limit better outcomes. Decisions made under uncertainty can also cause hidden issues to be ignored in favor of more visible or familiar problems.

Biases that reinforce group cohesion can contribute to groupthink and the exclusion of diverse perspectives. Reducing cognitive dissonance may also block honest reflection, while preserving core worldviews can make it harder to incorporate new evidence that could improve decision quality.

Solutions

To manage the drawbacks of biases while preserving potential benefits, decision makers should actively evaluate assumptions, seek diverse perspectives, and test beliefs against evidence.

Techniques such as structured reflection, checklists, and pre-mortem exercises can make possible biases more visible and adjustable.

Encouraging flexibility, questioning entrenched views, and treating beliefs as probabilistic rather than absolute can reduce the risk of overcommitment to flawed judgments while retaining the efficiency and coherence that shortcuts can provide.

Types of Biases

Bias Definition Helpful Harmful
Confirmation Bias Favoring information that supports your pre-existing beliefs while ignoring contradictory evidence. May help experts quickly filter information in familiar situations. Can block new understanding.
Sunk Cost Bias Continuing to invest in a decision due to prior investments. May coincide with persistence when evidence predicts that the investment will ultimately pay off. Harmful when future evidence suggests that continued investment will not pay off.
Overconfidence Bias The overestimation of one's skills or abilities. Can encourage quick action, especially in competitive environments where risk-taking creates opportunities. Can lead to underestimating risks or failing to create backup plans.
Anchoring Bias Focusing too much on the first piece of information encountered, or the "anchor," when making decisions. A legitimate anchor can provide a useful starting point in an ambiguous situation and speed up judgment. An irrelevant or misleading anchor can prevent adequate adjustment when new information emerges.
Loyalty Bias Staying loyal to a group or organization. Can contribute to long-term team building and group stability. Can protect underperformance, unethical behavior, or poor decisions.

Why This Matters in EEBDM

Heuristics and biases are unavoidable parts of human decision making. The goal is not to eliminate them, but to recognize when they are influencing our decisions and determine whether they are helping or hurting the quality of our reasoning.

Effective decision makers understand that fast thinking can be useful, but they also know when a situation requires slower, more deliberate analysis.

Ask yourself: What information am I noticing? What am I overlooking? Why am I drawn toward this particular conclusion?

By examining what information we notice, what we overlook, and why we are drawn toward particular conclusions, we can make more thoughtful and evidence-based decisions.
