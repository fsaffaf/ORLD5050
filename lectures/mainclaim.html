Understanding Claims

ORLD 5050 | Argument Skills

Learning Focus

Learn what a claim is, how to recognize one, and how to develop a clear, specific, and researchable claim related to your major.

1. What Is a Claim?

A claim is a statement that expresses what someone believes, thinks should happen, or wants others to accept.

A claim goes beyond simply naming a subject. It tells the reader what the person is saying about that subject.

Imagine that a company is deciding whether employees should be allowed to work from home.

Topic
Working from home
Claim
Employees should be allowed to work from home two days each week.

The topic tells us what the discussion is about. The claim tells us the position the person takes on that topic.

Another person might disagree and make a different claim:

Claim
Employees should work in the office five days each week.

Both statements are claims because they express positions that someone could agree or disagree with.

When You Read a Statement, Ask:

What is this person saying about the topic?

If the statement expresses a position that someone could accept or challenge, it may be a claim.

2. What Makes a Clear Claim?

A useful claim has three important features.

1. It Is Clear

The reader should understand what the person is saying. Avoid statements that are too vague or confusing.

Unclear
Technology is good.
Clearer
Companies should use technology to make employee training easier to access.

The second statement explains what the person thinks companies should do.

2. It Takes a Position

A claim expresses a point of view. Someone could agree or disagree with it.

Topic Only
Cybersecurity training
Claim
Companies should require all employees to complete cybersecurity training every year.

The first example names a subject. The second expresses a position about that subject.

3. It Can Be Discussed or Supported

A useful claim gives people something specific to consider. The person making the claim should be able to explain why it makes sense.

Too Vague
Employee training is important.
Stronger
Companies should provide cybersecurity training to all new employees before they begin working with customer information.

The stronger statement identifies a specific action that a company could consider. Someone could discuss whether the action is necessary, practical, or effective.

A claim does not have to be complicated. A short, direct statement is often easier to understand and evaluate than a long, complicated one.

3. What Is the Difference Between a Topic and a Claim?

A topic identifies what someone is discussing. A claim expresses what someone thinks about that topic.

Topic Claim
Employee training Companies should provide cybersecurity training to all new employees.
Remote work Employees should be allowed to work from home two days each week.
Workplace communication Managers should give employees feedback every month.
Artificial intelligence Companies should allow employees to use approved AI tools for routine tasks.
Employee schedules Employees should have flexible start times.

Notice that the topics are short labels. The claims express specific positions about those topics.

Knowing the topic is only the beginning. To understand someone's point of view, you need to identify what the person is saying about it.

4. Is Every Statement a Claim?

No. Some statements provide information, ask questions, or simply name a subject. Others express a position.

Statement What Does It Do?
The company has 500 employees. Provides information that can be checked.
How many employees work remotely? Asks a question.
Remote work Names a topic.
The company should allow employees to work remotely. Expresses a position.
The company has a remote-work policy. States something that can be checked.

A statement can be meaningful and specific without expressing a position that needs to be defended.

Claims Often Answer Questions Such As:

  • What should happen?
  • What should someone believe?
  • What is the best choice?
  • What should change?
  • What is the right way to approach a problem?

Claims do not always use the word should. For example, the statement "Remote work improves employee productivity" expresses a position about the effects of remote work. A reader could ask whether the statement is accurate and what information supports it.

Remember
A claim is not simply any sentence. It expresses a point that someone wants others to accept.

5. How Can You Identify a Claim?

When reading something, follow these steps.

  1. Identify the topic.
    Ask yourself, "What is this person talking about?"
  2. Look for a position.
    Ask yourself, "What is this person saying about the topic?"
  3. Check whether the statement expresses a point that could be challenged.
    Ask yourself, "Could someone reasonably disagree with this statement or ask for an explanation?"
  4. State the claim in your own words.
    Try to explain the person's central point in one clear sentence.
Important
You may need to read the entire passage to identify the claim. Do not assume that the first sentence always expresses the person's main point.

Practice Example

A technology company is deciding whether to change its employee training program. Currently, employees complete training only when they are hired. The company should require employees to complete cybersecurity training every year. Regular training would give employees opportunities to review security practices and learn about new threats.

Answer these questions:

  1. What is the topic of the passage?
  2. What position does the writer take?
  3. Which sentence most clearly expresses the writer's claim?

Answers

  1. Topic: Employee cybersecurity training.
  2. Position: Employees should receive cybersecurity training every year.
  3. Claim: The company should require employees to complete cybersecurity training every year.

The passage includes several details, but the claim identifies the specific action the writer wants the company to take.

6. How Do You Decide What Claim to Write About?

Sometimes the hardest part of writing a claim is deciding what you want to say. You may know your general subject, but you may not have a specific position in mind yet.

You do not need to have everything figured out before you begin. Start with something related to your major, identify a question or problem that interests you, and decide what you think should happen or what you believe is true.

Step 1: Start with Your Major

Choose a subject related to your field of study.

Cybersecurity

  • Employee security training
  • Protecting customer information
  • Password requirements
  • Artificial intelligence and security risks
  • Responding to cyberattacks

Organizational Leadership

  • Employee motivation
  • Leadership styles
  • Workplace communication
  • Employee performance evaluations
  • Flexible work schedules

Data Analytics

  • Using data to make business decisions
  • Data privacy
  • Predictive analytics
  • Data visualization
  • Using artificial intelligence to analyze data

You do not need to choose the most complicated subject. Choose something you want to understand better or think is important in your field.

Step 2: Narrow Your Topic

A broad topic can be difficult to write about because it includes too many different ideas. Narrowing your topic helps you focus on one specific issue.

Broad Topic More Focused Topic
Cybersecurity Employee cybersecurity training
Leadership How managers give employees feedback
Data analytics Using employee data to predict turnover

The more focused topic gives you a clearer starting point. However, even a more focused topic may need further narrowing before it becomes manageable.

Consider This Example

Too broad: Cybersecurity

More focused: Cybersecurity in businesses

More specific: Employee cybersecurity training

A specific issue: Whether businesses should require annual cybersecurity training for employees who handle customer information

Each step narrows the subject. Instead of trying to cover everything about cybersecurity, you can focus on one practice and consider whether it is a good idea.

You do not always need to narrow your topic as much as the final example. The goal is to identify a specific issue you can realistically explore in one course assignment.

Step 3: Identify a Question or Problem

Think about what people in your field need to decide, understand, or improve.

Ask yourself:

  • What is a problem that people in my field face?
  • What could be done better?
  • What decision should an organization make?
  • What practice should an organization change or keep?
  • What information would help people make better decisions?
  • What is something about my field that I would like to understand better?

For example, if you are interested in employee cybersecurity training, you might ask:

Should companies require employees to complete cybersecurity training every year?

This question gives you a specific issue to consider.

Step 4: Decide What You Think

Once you have a topic and a question, consider your own position.

You might believe that companies should require annual cybersecurity training. Or you might think that training should be required only for employees who handle sensitive information.

Either position could become a claim.

Position A
Companies should require all employees to complete cybersecurity training every year.
Position B
Companies should provide more intensive cybersecurity training to employees who handle sensitive information.

Both statements communicate a clear position. You do not have to choose the same position as someone else, and you do not need to have all the answers yet.

You can also explore a question about what is true rather than what should happen. For example, you might investigate whether flexible work schedules are associated with higher employee satisfaction.

Step 5: Turn Your Idea into a Claim

Write one sentence that communicates your position.

Cybersecurity

  • Topic: Employee cybersecurity training
  • Question: Should companies require annual training?
  • Claim: Companies should require all employees to complete cybersecurity training every year.

Organizational Leadership

  • Topic: Employee feedback
  • Question: How often should managers give employees feedback?
  • Claim: Managers should provide employees with formal performance feedback at least once every three months.

Data Analytics

  • Topic: Business decisions
  • Question: How should companies use data when making decisions?
  • Claim: Companies should use data analytics to evaluate business decisions rather than relying only on managerial intuition.
These are examples, not answers you must use. Choose a subject that interests you and develop your own position.

7. Is Your Topic Manageable and Searchable?

Choosing a topic is not just about finding something interesting. You also need to choose something you can realistically explore and find information about.

A topic may sound important but still be too large to address in one assignment. For example, explaining how cybersecurity affects every business around the world would require far more time and information than you have available.

A strong topic gives you enough to explore without requiring you to solve an entire industry's problems.

1. Choose Something You Can Realistically Address

Could I meaningfully explore this issue in one assignment, or would I need an entire book to cover it?

Some topics involve too many questions, groups, or problems to address at once.

Too Large to Manage Easily More Manageable
How can we eliminate cybercrime? Should businesses require employees to complete cybersecurity training annually?
How can leaders make every employee happy? Should managers offer employees more frequent performance feedback?
How can data analytics solve business problems? Should companies use employee turnover data to identify departments that may need additional support?
How will artificial intelligence change the entire economy? Should organizations allow employees to use approved AI tools for routine administrative tasks?

The more manageable topics focus on a particular decision, practice, or problem.

You are not expected to solve cybersecurity, fix every workplace, or explain the future of technology. You are exploring one issue and developing a clear position about it.

2. Make Sure You Can Find Information

A topic may be specific and interesting but still be difficult to research.

For example, you might want to argue that your employer should change its cybersecurity policies because employees are making too many mistakes. Investigating that claim might require access to private security reports, internal company policies, employee records, or confidential incident data.

You may not have permission to access that information. Even if you work for the organization, you should not assume that you can use its private information for a class assignment.

Instead, choose a topic you can explore using information that is publicly available, such as:

  • Published research articles
  • Government reports and statistics
  • Public company reports
  • Professional and industry publications
  • Publicly available case studies
  • Information published by recognized professional organizations

For example, instead of trying to prove that employees at your company frequently fall for phishing emails, you could explore whether organizations should conduct regular phishing-awareness training using published research and publicly available reports.

Important
Do not choose a topic that requires confidential company information, private employee records, or unauthorized access to internal systems. You should be able to investigate your topic without requesting special access to an employer's data.

3. Test Your Topic Before You Commit

Before settling on a claim, spend a few minutes searching for information about your topic. You do not need to conduct all your research yet. You are simply checking whether useful information appears to be available.

Try searching Google Scholar, your university library, or a reputable organization's website.

Use search terms that describe your specific issue.

Example: Cybersecurity

  • Broad search: cybersecurity
  • More focused search: employee cybersecurity training
  • More useful search: research on effectiveness of employee cybersecurity training

Example: Organizational Leadership

  • Broad search: leadership
  • More focused search: manager feedback and employee performance
  • More useful search: research on effects of manager feedback on employee performance

Example: Data Analytics

  • Broad search: data analytics
  • More focused search: predictive analytics and employee turnover
  • More useful search: research on using predictive analytics to identify employee turnover risk

Look for evidence that researchers or professionals have studied the issue. Check whether you can find articles, reports, or other sources that could help you understand the topic.

If your searches produce little useful information, try changing your search terms or choosing a related topic.

A topic is not researchable just because you can find a few websites mentioning it. You need a reasonable path toward finding credible information that can help you evaluate your position.

4. Use Four Questions to Evaluate Your Topic

Before choosing your final topic, ask yourself:

  1. Specific: Can I describe the exact issue I want to explore in one sentence?
  2. Manageable: Can I address this issue within the scope of one course assignment?
  3. Searchable: Can I find credible, publicly available information about this issue?
  4. Appropriate: Can I research this issue without needing confidential company information or unauthorized access to private data?

If you answer no to any of these questions, revise your topic before moving forward.

Example: From a Broad Topic to a Researchable Claim

Imagine that a student majoring in Data Analytics wants to write about artificial intelligence.

Starting Topic
Artificial intelligence

This topic is too broad. It includes many technologies, industries, and possible problems.

Narrower Topic
Artificial intelligence in the workplace

This is an improvement, but it still covers many different issues.

More Focused Topic
Using AI tools to make employee performance decisions

This identifies a more specific issue, but it may still involve questions about fairness, accuracy, privacy, and management.

Manageable Topic
Whether organizations should use AI tools to help evaluate employee performance

This identifies a particular organizational decision that the student can explore using published research and professional sources.

Possible Claim
Organizations should not use AI tools as the sole basis for employee performance evaluations.

The student now has a focused position to investigate. The student can look for research about the accuracy, fairness, and limitations of AI-based evaluations without needing access to private employee records.

The student may discover information that changes or strengthens the original position. That is a normal part of research.

8. How Do You Write and Check Your Claim?

Once you have selected a topic, identified a question, and checked that information is available, write one sentence that communicates your position.

Before finalizing your claim, ask yourself:

  • Is my point easy to understand?
  • Have I taken a clear position?
  • Is my statement specific enough for someone to understand what I mean?
  • Could someone reasonably disagree with my position?
  • Can I find credible information that will help me evaluate my position?
  • Can I explain why someone should accept my position?

If you can answer yes to these questions, you have a useful starting point.

Your first claim does not have to be perfect.

Writing often involves exploring an idea, learning more about it, and revising what you think. You do not need to prove your claim before you begin, but you should be willing to examine information that might support or challenge your initial position.

9. Why Do Claims Matter?

Claims help people communicate their positions clearly. They are especially important when people need to make decisions, solve problems, or recommend changes at work.

Imagine that a manager says:

"We need to improve employee training."

The manager has identified a concern, but the statement does not explain what should change.

Now imagine that the manager says:

"We should require all new employees to complete cybersecurity training before accessing customer information."

The second statement identifies a specific action. Other people can now consider the proposal, ask questions, and discuss whether it is a good decision.

A clear claim helps people understand exactly what is being proposed or discussed. Without one, a conversation can become unfocused because people may not know exactly what point they are considering.

10. Key Takeaways

  • A claim is a statement that expresses a position someone wants others to accept.
  • A topic identifies what someone is discussing.
  • A clear claim is understandable, specific, and open to discussion.
  • A useful claim gives the reader a clear idea of the position being presented.
  • A good topic is related to your major, manageable within the assignment, and searchable using credible sources.
  • You should not depend on confidential company information or private employee records to investigate your topic.
  • Your initial claim can change as you learn more about the issue.

Before You Begin Writing

Ask yourself:

If I could communicate only one point about this topic, what would I want my reader to understand or accept?

Start with a topic related to your major. Narrow it to one specific issue, check whether credible information is available, and write one sentence that expresses your position.

Your goal is not to choose the biggest topic. Your goal is to choose a specific issue about which you have something meaningful to say and can realistically find information to explore.
