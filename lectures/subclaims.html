Understanding Subclaims

A useful way to think about arguments is that real-world decisions are rarely supported by just one reason. Most strong arguments have one main claim that is supported by several subclaims. Each subclaim can then be developed into its own Toulmin argument with evidence, warrants, backing, qualifiers, counterarguments, and rebuttals.

For example, imagine that you are arguing:

Main Claim: Single-use plastics should be banned.

This claim is broad. To fully justify it, you might divide it into three supporting subclaims:

  1. Single-use plastics harm the environment.
  2. Single-use plastics create economic costs for society.
  3. Practical alternatives to single-use plastics are available.

Each of these subclaims can then be supported with its own Toulmin argument.

Complex Arguments with Multiple Subclaims

Many strong arguments follow a hierarchical structure:

  • Main Claim → The overall position or decision.
  • Subclaims → Major reasons supporting the main claim.
  • Toulmin Arguments → Each subclaim is supported using evidence, warrants, backing, qualifiers, counterarguments, and rebuttals.

Visualized, the structure looks like this: 

Main Claim
│
├── Subclaim 1
│    └── Toulmin Argument
│
├── Subclaim 2
│    └── Toulmin Argument
│
└── Subclaim 3
     └── Toulmin Argument

This approach helps decision makers organize complex reasoning and demonstrate that their conclusion is supported from multiple angles.

Understanding Subclaims

A main claim is the overall position you want your audience to accept. Subclaims are the individual reasons that explain why someone should accept that main claim.

A strong argument often includes three subclaims. Each subclaim should provide a different reason for accepting the main claim rather than repeating the same idea in different words.

Visualized, the structure looks like this:

Together, the three subclaims support the overall position from three distinct angles.

Example 1: Environmental Policy

Main Claim

Single-use plastics should be banned.

Three subclaims could support this position:

Subclaim 1: Environmental Protection

Single-use plastics contribute to pollution that harms wildlife and natural ecosystems.

Subclaim 2: Economic Costs

Reducing single-use plastics could lower the public costs associated with waste collection and pollution cleanup.

Subclaim 3: Alternative Products

Reusable and other alternative products can replace many single-use plastics in everyday situations.

These subclaims address three different considerations: environmental protection, economic costs, and the availability of alternatives.

Example 2: School Policy

Main Claim

Students should wear school uniforms.

Three subclaims could support this position:

Subclaim 1: Social Differences

School uniforms can reduce visible clothing-related differences in students' socioeconomic backgrounds.

Subclaim 2: School Safety

School uniforms can make it easier for staff to identify people who are not wearing the school's required attire.

Subclaim 3: Student Distractions

School uniforms can reduce pressure on students to compete over clothing and fashion trends.

These subclaims focus on three different issues: social differences, school safety, and clothing-related distractions.

Example 3: Cybersecurity

Main Claim

Organizations should require employees to complete regular cybersecurity training.

Three subclaims could support this position:

Subclaim 1: Threat Recognition

Regular cybersecurity training can help employees recognize phishing attempts and other common security threats.

Subclaim 2: Risk Reduction

Regular training can reduce the likelihood that employee mistakes will expose organizational information.

Subclaim 3: Incident Response

Regular training can help employees respond appropriately when they suspect a cybersecurity incident.

These subclaims address three distinct goals: recognizing threats, reducing security risks, and responding to incidents.

Example 4: Data Analytics

Main Claim

Organizations should use data analytics to guide business decisions.

Three subclaims could support this position:

Subclaim 1: Decision Accuracy

Data analytics can help decision makers identify patterns that may be overlooked when relying only on intuition.

Subclaim 2: Resource Allocation

Data analytics can help organizations identify where employees, time, and financial resources may be needed most.

Subclaim 3: Performance Monitoring

Data analytics can help organizations track performance over time and identify areas that may need improvement.

These subclaims focus on three different business applications: improving decisions, allocating resources, and monitoring performance.

Example 5: Workplace Leadership

Main Claim

Organizations should provide leadership training for managers.

Three subclaims could support this position:

Subclaim 1: Employee Communication

Leadership training can help managers communicate expectations and feedback more effectively.

Subclaim 2: Conflict Management

Leadership training can help managers address workplace disagreements constructively.

Subclaim 3: Employee Development

Leadership training can help managers identify opportunities to develop employees' skills and professional abilities.

These subclaims address three distinct leadership responsibilities: communication, conflict management, and employee development.

How to Evaluate Your Subclaims

A strong set of subclaims provides three different reasons for accepting the same main claim. Compare the following examples.

Weak Structure: Repeating the Same Idea

Main Claim: Organizations should provide leadership training.

  1. Leadership training improves leadership.
  2. Leadership training makes managers better leaders.
  3. Leadership training strengthens leadership skills.

These subclaims overlap substantially. They express nearly the same idea without identifying three distinct reasons.

Strong Structure: Three Different Reasons

Main Claim: Organizations should provide leadership training.

  1. Leadership training can improve managers' communication with employees.
  2. Leadership training can help managers resolve workplace conflicts.
  3. Leadership training can help managers develop employees' professional skills.

Each subclaim addresses a different aspect of leadership. Together, they provide a broader explanation for why an organization should invest in leadership training.

Apply This to Your Own Main Claim

When developing your argument, ask yourself:

Why should someone accept my main claim?

Identify three distinct answers. Each answer should become a subclaim that supports your overall position.

Before moving forward, check your work:

  • Does each subclaim support the main claim?
  • Does each subclaim provide a different reason?
  • Are the subclaims specific enough to investigate through research?
  • Do the three subclaims work together to support the overall position?

In Ethical, Evidence-Based Decision Making (EEBDM), this structure helps you organize a complex position into three clear, distinct reasons. Your subclaims establish the major points your overall argument needs to address.
