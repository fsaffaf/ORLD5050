Understanding Subclaims

A useful way to think about arguments is that real-world decisions are rarely supported by just one reason. Most strong arguments have one main claim that is supported by several subclaims. Each subclaim can then be developed into its own Toulmin argument with evidence, warrants, backing, qualifiers, counterarguments, and rebuttals.

For example, imagine that you are arguing:

Main Claim: Single-use plastics should be banned.

This claim is broad. To fully justify it, you might divide it into three supporting subclaims:

  1. Single-use plastics harm the environment.
  2. Single-use plastics create economic costs for society.
  3. Practical alternatives to single-use plastics are available.

Each of these subclaims can then be supported with its own Toulmin argument.

Complex Arguments with Multiple Subclaims

Many strong arguments follow a hierarchical structure:

  • Main Claim → The overall position or decision.
  • Subclaims → Major reasons supporting the main claim.
  • Toulmin Arguments → Each subclaim is supported using evidence, warrants, backing, qualifiers, counterarguments, and rebuttals.

Visualized, the structure looks like this: 

Main Claim
│
├── Subclaim 1
│    └── Toulmin Argument
│
├── Subclaim 2
│    └── Toulmin Argument
│
└── Subclaim 3
     └── Toulmin Argument

This approach helps decision makers organize complex reasoning and demonstrate that their conclusion is supported from multiple angles.

Example

Main Claim

Single-use plastics should be banned.

This is the overall position you want your audience to accept.

Subclaim 1: Single-Use Plastics Harm the Environment

Component Example
Subclaim Single-use plastics cause significant environmental harm.
Evidence Plastic waste pollutes oceans, waterways, and ecosystems.
Warrant Products that cause substantial environmental damage should be restricted when possible.
Backing Scientific studies show that plastic pollution harms wildlife and ecosystems worldwide.
Qualifier In most situations where alternatives exist.
Counterargument Some plastic products are necessary for sanitation and safety.
Rebuttal Targeted exemptions can address these situations without allowing widespread unnecessary plastic use.

Subclaim 2: Single-Use Plastics Create Economic Costs

Component Example
Subclaim Single-use plastics impose significant economic costs on society.
Evidence Governments spend millions of dollars each year on waste management and environmental cleanup.
Warrant When a product creates substantial public costs, policymakers should consider reducing its use.
Backing Economic studies show that pollution cleanup and waste disposal create financial burdens for taxpayers.
Qualifier In many communities affected by plastic waste.
Counterargument Plastic products are inexpensive and convenient for consumers.
Rebuttal Short-term convenience may not outweigh long-term public expenses associated with pollution and waste management.

Subclaim 3: Viable Alternatives Exist

Component Example
Subclaim Effective alternatives to single-use plastics are available.
Evidence Reusable containers, paper products, and biodegradable materials are increasingly common.
Warrant If safer alternatives can achieve the same purpose, continued reliance on a harmful product is less justified.
Backing Many businesses and countries have successfully adopted alternative packaging materials.
Qualifier In most consumer applications.
Counterargument Alternatives may be more expensive or less practical in some situations.
Rebuttal Costs often decrease as adoption increases, and exceptions can be made for situations where alternatives are not yet feasible.

Putting It All Together

When combined, the three subclaims support the main claim:

Main Claim:
Single-use plastics should be banned.

Supporting Subclaims:

  1. They harm the environment.
  2. They create economic costs.
  3. Practical alternatives exist.

Because each subclaim is supported by its own Toulmin argument, the overall argument becomes much stronger than relying on a single reason alone.

A Quick Test for Strong Subclaims

A good set of subclaims should pass this test:

Weak Structure

Main Claim: Students should wear school uniforms.

  • Uniforms create equality.
  • Uniforms create fairness.
  • Uniforms reduce differences.

These subclaims are largely saying the same thing.

Strong Structure

Main Claim: Students should wear school uniforms.

  • Uniforms can reduce visible socioeconomic differences among students.
  • Uniforms can improve school safety by making unauthorized visitors easier to identify.
  • Uniforms can reduce distractions related to clothing and fashion competition.

These subclaims support the same claim but provide three distinct reasons for accepting it.

As you develop your own argument, try to make each subclaim answer a different version of the question:

"Why should someone accept my main claim?"

If all three subclaims provide different answers to that question, you are usually on the right track.

Why This Matters in EEBDM

In Ethical, Evidence-Based Decision Making (EEBDM), complex decisions often require consideration of multiple factors. Rather than relying on one piece of evidence or one reason, decision makers typically build a case using several supporting subclaims.

Each subclaim should be supported with evidence and defended against objections through the Toulmin model. This structure helps ensure that decisions are not only persuasive but also logically sound, ethically justified, and supported by evidence.

Key Takeaways

  • Strong arguments often contain one main claim supported by several subclaims.
  • Each subclaim can be developed into its own Toulmin argument.
  • Subclaims should provide distinct reasons for accepting the main claim.
  • Evidence, warrants, backing, qualifiers, counterarguments, and rebuttals can strengthen each subclaim.
  • Multiple well-developed subclaims help decision makers build more complete and defensible arguments.
