Understanding the Main Claim of an Argument

ORLD 5050 | Argument Skills

Learning Focus

Learn how to identify the central point of an argument and understand how reasons and evidence support it.

1. What Is a Main Claim?

A main claim is the central point that a person wants others to believe or accept. It tells the reader what the writer thinks is true, what should happen, or what decision should be made.

Think of the main claim as the answer to this question:

What is the writer trying to convince me of?

Every argument needs a main claim. Without one, the reader may not understand what the writer is trying to prove.

Example

Imagine that a manager at a technology company wants employees to work from home two days each week.

Main Claim

Employees should be allowed to work from home two days each week.

This sentence tells us exactly what the manager wants the company to do. It is the main claim because the rest of the argument should explain why the company should accept this idea.

2. What Makes a Strong Main Claim?

A strong main claim has three important features.

1. Clear

The reader should understand what the writer is saying. Avoid statements that are too general or confusing.

2. Takes a Position

The writer makes a point that someone could agree or disagree with.

3. Can Be Supported

The writer can explain why the claim makes sense and provide information to support it.

Consider these examples:

Statement Assessment Why?
Technology is important. Too general It does not take a clear position.
Many companies use technology. Not a strong claim It is a statement of fact, not a clear position to defend.
Companies should provide cybersecurity training to all employees. Strong claim It takes a clear position that can be supported with reasons and evidence.
Cybersecurity training is good. Needs improvement It is too vague. The writer should explain what is good about it or what should be done.

A main claim does not need to be complicated. A simple, clear statement is often stronger than a long sentence filled with difficult words.

3. How Is a Main Claim Different from a Topic?

A topic tells the reader what the argument is about. A main claim tells the reader what the writer believes about that topic.

Topic: Cybersecurity training

Main claim: Companies should require all employees to complete cybersecurity training every year.

The topic identifies the subject. The main claim gives the writer's position on that subject.

Knowing the topic is not enough. When you create an argument, you must decide what you want your audience to believe or do about that topic.

4. How Is a Main Claim Different from a Supporting Reason?

A main claim is the central point of the argument. A supporting reason explains why the audience should accept that point.

Main Claim

Companies should require all employees to complete cybersecurity training every year.

Supporting Reasons
  • Regular training can help employees recognize phishing emails.
  • Training can help employees learn how to protect private company information.
  • Employees who understand security risks may be less likely to make costly mistakes.

Notice that the supporting reasons connect back to the main claim. Each one explains why the company should require training.

A supporting reason is not the same as evidence. A reason explains why the claim makes sense. Evidence provides information that helps show whether that reason is true.

Reason: Training can help employees recognize phishing emails.

Evidence: Company records show that employees who completed the training correctly identified more test phishing emails than employees who had not completed it.

The evidence makes the reason more believable.

5. How Do You Find the Main Claim in an Argument?

When reading an argument, use these four steps.

  1. Identify the topic.
    Ask yourself, "What is this argument about?"
  2. Look for the writer's position.
    Ask yourself, "What does the writer think should happen, or what does the writer want me to believe?"
  3. Look at the reasons.
    Ask yourself, "What points does the writer use to explain or defend this position?"
  4. Find the statement that connects the argument.
    The main claim is the central point that the supporting reasons help defend. It may appear at the beginning, in the middle, or at the end of an argument.
Important

Do not assume that the first sentence is always the main claim. Read the entire argument before deciding.

Practice Example

A technology company should allow employees to work from home two days each week. Employees may have more time to focus on their work when they have fewer office distractions. Working from home can also reduce the time employees spend commuting. These changes may help employees manage their time and complete their work.

Now identify the parts of the argument.

  • Topic: Working from home
  • Main claim: The company should allow employees to work from home two days each week.
  • Supporting reason 1: Employees may have fewer distractions.
  • Supporting reason 2: Employees may spend less time commuting.
  • Overall purpose: To convince the company to allow employees to work from home two days each week.

The main claim gives the argument its direction. The supporting reasons explain why the company should consider the proposal.

6. What Is the Difference Between a Main Claim and a Fact?

A fact is a statement that can be checked to determine whether it is true or false. A main claim takes a position that the writer wants the audience to accept.

Type Example
Fact The company has 500 employees.
Main claim The company should provide cybersecurity training to all 500 employees.

The fact provides information about the company. The main claim recommends an action.

Arguments often use facts as evidence to support a claim. However, facts alone do not tell the reader what decision should be made. The writer must explain how the facts support the main claim.

7. Why Does the Main Claim Matter?

The main claim helps the writer stay focused. It also helps the reader understand the purpose of the argument.

Without a clear main claim, an argument may include many ideas without explaining how they fit together. The reasons may be interesting, but the reader may not understand what they are supposed to prove.

A clear main claim helps answer three questions:

  1. What does the writer want the audience to believe or do?
  2. What reasons support that position?
  3. What evidence helps show that the reasons are strong?

These questions are especially important when making decisions at work. For example, a manager may argue that a company should change its training program, hire more employees, or adopt a new technology. The main claim identifies the decision the manager wants others to consider.

8. Key Takeaways

Remember these points when reading or writing an argument:

  • The main claim is the central point the writer wants the audience to accept.
  • The topic tells the reader what the argument is about.
  • A supporting reason explains why the audience should accept the main claim.
  • Evidence provides information that supports a reason or claim.
  • A strong main claim is clear, takes a position, and can be supported with reasons and evidence.
  • Every supporting part of the argument should connect to the main claim.
Before You Begin Writing

If my reader remembers only one point from my argument, what do I want that point to be?

Your answer is a good starting place for your main claim.
